The mechanism of institutional bouncing

Every year, with the punctuality of the seasons, come the increases in mandatory health insurance premiums. With them come the explanations, justifications, and the political refrain: 'It is not up to us', 'We cannot do anything'. It is a mechanism that repeats cyclically, where competencies pass from one institutional level to another without the final bill changing its recipient.

Healthcare is a topic that explodes, but politics seems incapable of intervening on the deep causes. The debate often focuses on who is responsible: the Confederation, the Cantons, hospitals, doctors, or health insurance funds. In reality, while responsibilities bounce around, the economic weight always falls on the pockets of citizens. For many Swiss families, the premium is no longer a simple budget item, but a cost that directly impacts the quality of life, on par with rent, mortgage, taxes, energy, and transport.

The paradox is evident: there is continuous talk of cost containment, reforms, and efficiency. However, premiums increase punctually, and citizens are asked to tighten their belts. The serious question is not whether healthcare costs money, but why it costs so much and why no one truly intervenes on the causes, such as bureaucracy, duplicate structures, or inefficiencies. The citizen becomes the last link in the chain, paying premiums, taxes, and deductibles, sometimes giving up on care due to the high cost. Operational details

Analysis of System Responsibilities and Inefficiencies

The original text highlights a structural fragility of the Swiss healthcare system: the separation between defining competencies and assuming economic responsibility. When it comes to health, blame tends to be fragmented among multiple actors. The Confederation manages federal aspects, the Cantons local ones, but hospitals, doctors, and health insurance funds also contribute to cost trends. This fragmentation prevents a unified intervention on the main causes of healthcare inflation.

There are concrete questions that politics should address transparently. Which services can be rationalized? How much do bureaucracy, duplicated structures, and special interests impact system inefficiencies? Currently, the system seems to continuously generate new costs, but there is a lack of willingness to intervene at the root. This creates a sense of powerlessness in citizens, who perceive a lack of concrete protection. Citizens pay several times: premiums, taxes, deductibles, and co-payments. This multiplication of fiscal and contributory burdens weighs on purchasing power and limits families' economic freedom of choice.

The public reaction to these increases is described as strangely weak. Marginal details are discussed for weeks, people get indignant about minor issues, but when faced with a problem affecting the budget of hundreds of thousands of people every year, resignation and silence prevail. People have become accustomed to increases, explanations, and the shifting of responsibilities. This habituation to the unacceptable is perhaps the real problem blocking change. It is not enough to accept that healthcare costs money; it is necessary to demand clarity on where the money is spent and who is accountable. The absence of a single, identifiable responsible party makes it difficult to exercise democratic control over healthcare spending. Cost of Living Comparison

Key points

Concrete proposal: tax deductibility of premiums

The source proposes a concrete and immediately applicable measure to alleviate the burden of increases: making mandatory health insurance premiums fully tax-deductible. This solution would not solve the root problem, nor would it reduce overall healthcare costs, nor would it replace necessary structural reforms. However, it would have an immediate effect: it would reduce the tax burden on families and citizens, returning to them a portion of what they are currently forced to allocate to mandatory coverage. The central argument is simple: we wouldn't want to tax the same income twice, would we? If healthcare costs increase, deductibility would partially offset this increase, maintaining more purchasing power in the hands of taxpayers.

For residents in Switzerland, this measure would require careful verification in their tax declaration. It is crucial to assess how the deduction would integrate with current deductions for medical expenses and deductibles. Since federal direct tax and cantonal taxes operate on separate levels, the impact of deductibility varies depending on the canton and overall income. Citizens should consult their declaration to verify how healthcare expenses are already treated and if a regulatory change would bring a significant net benefit.

While awaiting potential legislative reforms, citizens can still optimize their current tax situation. It is advisable to check if one is entitled to cantonal subsidies for premium reduction, which can cover part of the cost. Furthermore, it is useful to compare offers from different health insurance providers ('cassa malati') to ensure being on the most advantageous plan possible. Frequently Asked Questions
Why do compulsory health insurance premiums increase every year?
Increases in compulsory health insurance premiums are an annual phenomenon, accompanied by political explanations and justifications that often result in a 'It's not up to us'. This mechanism of rebounding responsibilities between the Confederation, Cantons, hospitals and sick funds prevents a unitary intervention on the root causes, leaving the economic burden to fall on the citizens. Health is a complex issue where the fragmentation of skills hinders efficiency and cost containment.
What is the impact of premium increases on the lives of Swiss families?
For many Swiss families, the health insurance premium is no longer a simple budget item, but a significant cost that directly affects the quality of life. This burden is comparable to essential expenses such as rent, mortgage, taxes, energy, and transportation. The multiplication of charges, including premiums, taxes, deductibles and shareholdings, weighs on purchasing power and limits freedom of economic choice, sometimes leading to the renunciation of treatment due to too high a cost.
What concrete measure is proposed to alleviate the burden of sickness premiums?
Full deductibility from taxes of compulsory health insurance premiums is proposed. This measure, while not solving the structural causes of the increases or reducing overall healthcare costs, would have an immediate effect: it would reduce the tax burden on families and citizens. The objective is to avoid taxing the same income twice, partially offsetting the increase in health costs and keeping more purchasing power in the hands of taxpayers.
