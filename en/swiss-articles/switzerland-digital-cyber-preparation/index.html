Switzerland a digital leader but lagging behind in cyber defense

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 28 September 2026

Switzerland leads digital competitiveness with 100/100, but is only 31st out of 66 for cyber preparedness (5,32/10); attacks have increased by 35% and 7 incidents have been recorded in the past year.

Digital leadership and the weak point

ZURICH - Switzerland leads the digital competitiveness ranking, but remains only in 31st place out of 66 when the metric changes and concerns preparedness to counter cyber threats. This is the paradox highlighted by a new international study conducted by Abilene Academy, an organization specializing in governance, risk and compliance.

The digital score is 100/100. In this ranking, Switzerland comes ahead of the United States, Sweden, Denmark and the Netherlands, which score 99,29, 95,42, 97,23 and 96,82 respectively. In terms of response to cyber threats, however, the result falls to 5,32 out of 10. The gap between the two figures is at the heart of the research: the drive for innovation does not automatically correspond to an equivalent defensive capacity.

The attack data

According to the study, in the past year Swiss organizations recorded 7 cyberattacks or political incidents. The report indicates 0,0777 incidents per 100.000 inhabitants, a figure higher than that recorded in France (0,0644), Germany (0,0478) and Italy (0,0255). The source also reports that attacks against Swiss organizations increased by 35% in one year: the comparison therefore concerns not only a position in the ranking, but also the evolution of the phenomenon.

The European comparison makes the gap even more pronounced. France, despite having 81,91 points in digital competitiveness, ranks 14th in preparedness. Germany reaches 84,25 points in competitiveness and 6,45 in the overall score; Italy totals 6,32. Thus Switzerland retains the best digital ecosystem among the major European economies, but the lowest score for cybersecurity preparedness.

Globally, Singapore leads the preparedness ranking with 8,53 out of 10, followed by the United Arab Emirates, Finland, Saudi Arabia and the United States. Switzerland remains mid-table for resilience to cyber threats. Henri Haenni, general director of Abilene Academy, sums up the distinction in one sentence:

«being at the forefront of digitalization is not enough to guarantee an economy’s resilience to cyber threats».

Haenni adds that cybersecurity must be integrated into governance and decision-making processes at all levels. For Switzerland, the priority identified by the study is to develop digital risk management alongside technological capabilities. Alexis Hirschhorn, CEO of ACUNA GRC, describes the same gap between world-class infrastructure and risk management that is not always aligned. The message of the study is therefore clear: digital leadership alone does not guarantee resilience.

Operational details

The gap between technology and resilience

For those who live or work in Switzerland, the most useful figure is not only digital leadership, but the separation between two capabilities that the study keeps distinct: innovating and withstanding cyber threats. The score of 100/100 measures digital competitiveness; the 5,32 out of 10, on the other hand, measures preparedness to counter cyber threats. They are not two ways of saying the same thing. The research shows that an advanced technological ecosystem does not automatically produce an equally ready response to incidents.

Comparison as a lens for interpretation

Comparison with neighboring countries avoids an overly simple explanation. France scores 81,91 points in digital competitiveness, therefore less than Switzerland, but ranks 14th in preparedness. Germany reaches 84,25 points and an overall score of 6,45; Italy scores 6,32. The rankings suggest that digital competitiveness and cyber resilience must be assessed separately. Looking only at the strength of the digital ecosystem is not enough to understand how ready an economy is to respond.

For a Swiss organization finding itself in the situation described, the practical consequence is a change in priorities. Investing in security technologies may be part of the response, but according to Alexis Hirschhorn, it does not by itself close the gap. Governance must become operational and must enter into daily decisions, beyond the logic of the annual audit. Risk management thus becomes a process responsibility, not an isolated control.

Henri Haenni takes the same reasoning beyond the IT department alone. As cyber threats increase and become more sophisticated, he says, cybersecurity must be integrated into governance and decision-making processes at all levels. For those working in a Swiss organization, the concrete point is to understand whether the ability to respond to incidents is considered a function of the organization as a whole or merely a technical issue. The study points toward the former interpretation.

This perspective is also useful to those examining the aziende che assumono or looking in the annunci di lavoro for the weight of digital skills: competitiveness and resilience do not coincide. They are two dimensions of the Swiss system that the research encourages us not to confuse.

Key points

Five steps for translating the study’s message

The operational message of the research can be translated into five steps for an organization that wants to align its digital level with its defense capability. These are governance steps, not a catalog of technologies: this is the distinction outlined by Abilene Academy and the two executives cited.

1. Separate the two measures. Assess digital competitiveness on the one hand and readiness to counter threats on the other; a result of 100/100 does not replace 5,32 out of 10. 2. Bring risk into governance. Cybersecurity should not be left solely to the IT department: it must be integrated into decision-making processes at all levels. 3. Make governance operational. The reference point is not only the annual audit; risk management must become part of everyday decisions. 4. Align management and technology. Digital risk management must evolve in step with technological capabilities; investing in new security technologies alone does not close the gap. 5. Build response capability. Resilience requires sound risk management and the ability to respond to incidents; according to Haenni, it is acquired through experience and cannot be decreed.

The question to bring into decision-making

Applied to a Swiss context, the sequence starts with a question: is the level of innovation progressing alongside digital risk management? If the answer is not aligned, the problem identified by the research cannot be solved by proclaiming technological primacy. It must be addressed in decision-making processes and in the ability to respond to incidents.

For those who live or work in the country, the most concrete action is therefore to view cybersecurity as a governance issue, not as a subject confined to IT. The criterion proposed by the study can be verified in organizational practice: does risk enter everyday decisions or does it appear only in the annual audit? The answer makes it possible to understand whether digital infrastructure is accompanied by risk management that is up to the task, without confusing a single investment with resilience.

The priority indicated for Switzerland remains to make digital risk management evolve in step with technological capabilities. This is a direction that carries even greater weight as threats increase and become more sophisticated.
