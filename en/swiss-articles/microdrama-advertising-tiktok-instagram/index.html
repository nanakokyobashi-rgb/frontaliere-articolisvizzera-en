In recent weeks, ads created with artificial intelligence have appeared on Instagram and TikTok, using sex scenes ranging from the bizarre to the fantastical. One video may show a werewolf’s bride transforming during intercourse; another tells the story of a woman’s pregnancy with a dragon prince. The scenes exploit the visual provocation that characterizes the format’s early days.

Behind this content are microdrama apps, platforms dedicated to short melodramatic series with sexual allusions. It is not just about the individual ad: behind the campaign is an entertainment model that moves from social media to a proprietary app.

The format that brings viewers along

The episodes are shot vertically, last one or two minutes and end with a twist. The apps are free to download, and the initial episodes are also free; to continue, however, users have to subscribe and pay. Sexuality is often merely the initial hook. The plot weaves together three elements: the forbidden, represented by relationships with nonhuman beings, authoritarian leaders, enemies or dangerous creatures; metamorphosis, which may consist of an impossible pregnancy, a changing body or a hidden supernatural nature; and the twist, when the partner turns out to be a king, monster, billionaire or father of an heir destined to change the world.

Paolo Attivissimo, a technology journalist and popular science communicator, identifies young people and women as the most interested audience. In the United States, according to the cited data, the bulk of the audience consists of women aged 25 to 55; monthly users number 66 million, about one in six Americans, while CandyJar’s audience is said to be 99 percent female.

On the Swiss front, the institutional step cited by the source concerns the Federal Council, which on Wednesday passed the LPCom. Bern aims to strengthen protections for young people online, ensure transparency for Google and Facebook, and block targeted advertising, which exploits behavioral tracking and is used by microdramas. The message is expected by June 2027.

Operational details

The business beyond the provocative image

Comparing the costs helps explain why microdramas have become an industry. Attivissimo sets a traditional sitcom side by side with a production of 50–75 episodes:

The source links the first figure to the sitcom’s production machinery: after success, actors ask for more money, while costume designers, catering, lighting technicians, sound engineers and set designers quickly drive up costs. The second format can be made with real actors or AI and condenses the story into very short vertical episodes.

AI, production and work

The landscape, the source says, changed this year with Seedance 2.0, a model that generates videos much more realistic than previous ones. The model therefore makes the artificial generation used in ads and series more credible.

Attivissimo nevertheless draws a distinction: major film production remains superior; the technology will instead replace mediocre actors. Good actors and highly specialized specialists will continue to work, while those who perform generic work are at greater risk.

The business model is completed by the shift from social media to proprietary apps. Social media show the suggestive scene; the app avoids app-store commissions and monetizes each individual episode with micropayments. According to Attivissimo, sex remains implicit: it is not shown and can thus stay within the limits or evade checks. The expert adds that some social platforms are complicit when a channel brings in millions of dollars, despite official prohibitions.

What it means for Switzerland

For the reader in Switzerland, the concrete consequence concerns targeted advertising. The source links microdramas to behavioral tracking and reports that the Federal Council aims to strengthen protections for young people online, ensure transparency for Google and Facebook, and stop that type of advertising. The dossier thus concerns how the ad reaches the user, not just the erotic content.

Attivissimo also envisions tailor-made stories, with a choice of emotion and eroticism, while systems insert the advertising. He cites Black Mirror as an already anticipated scenario and argues that AI, being unoriginal, will leave room for different niches, such as art-house films and vinyl records or CDs in music.

Key points

The practical path to reconstruct

To correctly read an advertisement of this kind, it is useful to separate four stages: the trailer, the app, the payment and the federal dossier.

1. Recognizing the initial signal

The first step is social media: Instagram or TikTok. The clues are an AI-generated video, a scene somewhere between sex and fantasy, and a few words charged with narrative or emotional tension. The cases cited are the werewolf's bride and the dragon prince's pregnant woman. The content does not necessarily tell the whole story: sexuality may be merely the initial hook, while the series develops themes of the forbidden, transformation and a twist.

2. Following the transition to the app

The second moment is the redirection. The advertisement takes the user away from social media, toward the proprietary app of the microdrama. The platforms cited are ReelShort, DramaBox and ShortMax. Initial access is free, and the first episodes also do not require payment. The progression is built around one- or two-minute vertical episodes, each ending with a twist.

3. Separating free access and spending

The third step is the financial one. To continue the series, one must subscribe and pay; the model also includes micropayments for each episode. Attivissimo indicates a cost of about 20 francs to watch an entire series, but points out that it can reach 200 dollars per month. The source associates the first amount with watching the entire series and the second with monthly subscriptions. This distinction helps avoid confusing free access to the app with free viewing of the entire catalog.

4. Keeping the federal deadline separate

The final step concerns the Swiss dossier, not the individual episode. The Federal Council approved the LPCom on Wednesday; the message is expected by June 2027. The stated objectives include greater protection for young people online, more transparency for Google and Facebook, and a ban on targeted advertising based on behavioral tracking. For the reader, this distinction avoids confusing how the apps work with the announced institutional phase.

The final check follows a precise sequence: advertisement on social media, transfer to the app, initial free episodes, a request for a subscription or micropayment and, in the public sphere, awaiting the federal message by the stated deadline. It is this chain, more than the individual fantasy scene, that explains how the microdrama turns attention into revenue and how the issue of targeted advertising entered the federal dossier.
