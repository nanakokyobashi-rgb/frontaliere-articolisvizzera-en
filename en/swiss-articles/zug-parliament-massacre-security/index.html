Zug, 25 years ago the massacre in the Parliament chamber

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 27 September 2026

On September 27, 2001, Friedrich Leibacher killed 14 people and wounded 15 in the Zug Parliament, firing 91 shots in 2 minutes and 34 seconds, leading to a change in security.

On September 27, 2001, shortly after 10:30 AM, an armed man entered the Zug Parliament chamber while 80 Grand Councillors and the entire cantonal government were gathered. Within two minutes and 34 seconds, Friedrich Leibacher fired 91 shots, killed 14 people and injured 15 others, before taking his own life.

The raid on the Government Building

Leibacher was 57 years old, a Swiss citizen, had no regular job, and had repeatedly faced legal problems. According to the reconstruction, he acted out of hatred towards the cantonal authorities, having planned the attack alone and well in advance. That morning, he appeared at the Government Building wearing a self-made police vest and a cap with the cantonal coat of arms. He entered the Grand Council chamber, where the session was underway, undisturbed.

He carried a Swiss army assault rifle, a pump-action shotgun, a pistol, a revolver, and a homemade bomb. The source specifies that all weapons had been legally acquired. Leibacher opened fire, shouting insults at the authorities; the deputies sought cover under the benches, and during the attack, he also detonated the explosive device.

The alarm was raised at 10:35 AM. The first responders arrived ten minutes later, at 10:45 AM. 14 ambulances, two Rega helicopters, about a hundred police officers, and 240 firefighters were mobilized to the scene.

Heinz Tännler's testimony

Heinz Tännler, a member of the UDC and currently the Director of Finance for the canton of Zug, was then sitting near the entrance door. He is the only still-serving member of the government or Parliament who experienced the attack. When Leibacher burst into the room, Tännler threw himself under a bench. Next to him was the liberal-radical deputy Martin Döbeli, severely wounded in the head. Tännler recounted that he could not help him. Döbeli died in the helicopter during transport to the hospital; Tännler, however, sustained minor injuries.

'I am not an anxious person. But that day I was afraid. Afraid of dying,' Tännler told the Keystone-ATS agency.

President of the Confederation Moritz Leuenberger spoke of an hour of dismay. In Bern, the President of the National Council, Peter Hess from Zug, prematurely adjourned the session, and the flags on the Federal Palace were lowered to half-mast. On October 1, the day of commemoration in Zug, Switzerland observed a minute of silence; SSR radios and televisions also fell silent. 25 years later, the attack remains the most serious in Swiss history.

Operational details

Parliamentary Security: Before and After

The Zug attack led to a noticeable change in the places where elected representatives meet. The source describes a "before" and "after": already on that day, several cantons strengthened controls; subsequently, security gates, alarm buttons, and cameras were introduced. The massacre was not confined to a local emergency but altered the protection of Swiss parliaments.

From the Federal Palace to the Cantons

The clearest comparison concerns the Federal Palace. Before the attack, it was freely accessible. Afterwards, metal detectors were installed, and baggage screening was introduced. Even today, approximately 100,000 annual visitors must identify themselves and undergo a check. Entry therefore includes identification and baggage screening.

In the cantons, the response was not uniform. Only Appenzell Innerrhoden, among those indicated by the source, did not deem intervention necessary. The measures listed — gates, alarm buttons, and cameras — show a common direction: controlling access, signaling danger, and monitoring spaces. The specifics of the measures, however, depend on cantonal choices.

Zug and Threat Management

In Zug, for three years following the massacre, the Grand Council met in a protected police building. It then returned to the renovated chamber, with new furnishings and access controls. The reopening was thus accompanied by a modification of the space and entry procedures.

Since 2013, most cantons have implemented a threat management system to identify potentially dangerous individuals in time. Zug does not yet have this system: an amendment to the police law that would introduce it is currently under consultation. The issue thus remains open regarding the transition from physical controls to the proactive assessment of threats.

For the public, the concrete effect is the distinction between free access and regulated access. At the Federal Palace, identification and baggage screening apply; in Zug, the return to the chamber was linked to new controls; in the cantons, threat management systems are not present everywhere in the same way. The source therefore documents a security model built after September 27, 2001.

Key points

What to check before visiting a parliament

The source makes it possible to derive a practical sequence for those who need to visit a parliamentary building or follow the evolution of cantonal rules. This is not an identical procedure throughout Switzerland: the measures have changed over time and the cantons have made different choices. The steps list the elements indicated for the Federal Palace and Zug.

Four useful steps

1. Identify the venue. If the destination is the Federal Palace, bear in mind that the building, once freely accessible, now requires visitors to provide identification and undergo baggage checks. The source indicates approximately 100’000 visitors per year subjected to these checks.

2. Prepare for the entrance check. At the Federal Palace, providing identification and undergoing a baggage check are part of the access procedure described. Cantonal parliaments may also have security gates, alarm buttons and cameras, measures introduced after the massacre.

3. Distinguish the situation in Zug. The Grand Council met for three years in a police-protected building and then returned to the renovated chamber, with new furnishings and access controls. Those following the canton must distinguish the protected venue used during the emergency from the subsequent renovated chamber.

4. Follow the legislative dossier. Since 2013, the majority of cantons have had a threat-management system. Zug does not yet have one; the amendment to the police law that should introduce it is under consultation. For the reader, the operational step is to distinguish between the system already in place in most cantons and the proposal still under consultation in Zug.

This reading avoids confusing three levels: the physical measures at the entrance, the temporary protection of the venue and the threat-management system. The first concerns metal detectors, baggage and identification at the Federal Palace; the second concerns the temporary relocation of the Grand Council of Zug; the third concerns the early identification of potentially dangerous people. These are different elements, even though they belong to the same evolution of parliamentary security.

When reading a cantonal update, the operational criterion is to look for which of the three levels is being modified. In Zug, the return to the renovated chamber concerns the venue; the amendment to the police law concerns the threat-management system. At the Federal Palace, metal detectors, baggage and identification concern access. This distinction makes it possible not to confuse a physical measure with a legislative proposal.
