The rest of Switzerland thinks in one way, while Ticino is moving in another direction according to the first surveys on the neutrality initiative that will bring citizens to the polls on September 27. The initiative appears to be oriented towards rejection with thirty-six percent of the national consensus, but in Ticino, based on the Tamedia survey, it collects fifty-two percent of the preferences. This dynamic highlights a divergence of views between German Switzerland and the territory south of the Alps, an aspect not unprecedented and already observed in recent federal votes. Operational details

Returning to the figures and analyzing the data collected more than a month before the vote, the political scientist of the University of Bern Fabio Wasserfallen invites us to take this counter-trend with a grain of salt, highlighting that Ticino's representation in proportion to the rest of the sample examined is rather small. Given this premise, the expert recalls that the initiative proposes a specific interpretation of neutrality and that, consequently, the vote is not expressed on neutrality itself but on the form that citizens prefer to adopt. Wasserfallen underlines how relevant the political brand of the initiative is, highlighting that the polls show that the proposal is clearly linked to the UDC and that voters take this element into account, as confirmed by the results divided according to the political parties of reference.

Frequently Asked Questions
When will the vote on the neutrality initiative take place?
The citizens' vote is scheduled for 27 September. The popular consultation will see those entitled express themselves on this initiative of federal scope, receiving the official documentation directly at their home in the manner established by the competent authorities.
What are the consensus percentages that emerged from the Tamedia surveys?
According to the Tamedia survey cited by the political scientist Fabio Wasserfallen of the University of Bern, the neutrality initiative receives thirty-six percent of the national consensus, while in Ticino support rises to fifty-two percent, highlighting a considerable territorial divergence.
What is the role of the UDC and Euroscepticism in this vote?
Political scientist Fabio Wasserfallen points out that the initiative has a clear link with the UDC and that voters take this political brand into account. In addition, Euroscepticism weighs on voting intentions in Ticino, which represents a solid bulwark and connects directly with the favourable orientation towards the initiative south of the Alps.
