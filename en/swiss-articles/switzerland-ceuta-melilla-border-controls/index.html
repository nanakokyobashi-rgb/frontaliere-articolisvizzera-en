After Ceuta and Melilla, border controls (cross-border guide)

Di Marco Ferrari · Published on 1 August 2026 · Updated on 25 September 2026

Tens of thousands of migrants have crossed the border into the Schengen area. The episode inflames the political debate, even in Switzerland.

Context

In brief

  • Tens of thousands of migrants have crossed the border into the Schengen area.
  • The incident is fueling political debate, even in Switzerland.
  • The Federal Council must introduce systematic border controls.

Key facts

  • What: Massive influx of migrants into the Schengen area.
  • When: Thursday, March 17, 2023.
  • Where: Spanish enclaves of Ceuta and Melilla, North Africa.
  • Who: Swiss Federal Council, Spanish Guardia Civil.
  • Amount: Around 49,000 arrivals in Ceuta and hundreds in Melilla.

The Spanish enclaves of Ceuta and Melilla in North Africa are under pressure from a massive influx of migrants. On Thursday, thousands of people reached Ceuta by swimming or walking from Morocco, thus entering the Schengen area, which also includes Switzerland. On Friday, the Spanish Guardia Civil reported 49,000 arrivals, not counting the hundreds of migrants who entered Melilla. The government has sent the army for support.

The causes are not yet fully clear. There is talk of a decision by the Spanish Supreme Court that migrants attempting to reach Ceuta or Melilla by swimming cannot be turned back without further reasons. There is also speculation about a possible deliberate easing of controls by Morocco.

In Switzerland, the events in North Africa are sparking very different reactions politically.

"No one should risk their life to be protected"

Socialist National Councillor Michelle Dünki-Bättig speaks of shocking images: "People are trying to swim to a European border and dying in the attempt." She argues that the army and fences do not solve the problem; instead, they make escape routes even more dangerous. Europe needs safe and legal access routes and an effective sea rescue system.

Switzerland must also act, adds Dünki-Bättig. She calls for the reintroduction of asylum in embassies, abolished in 2012, which allowed asylum applications to be submitted at a Swiss representation abroad. The humanitarian visa, she says, is not an equivalent substitute: "No one who needs protection should have to risk their life just to apply for asylum."

"They are not refugees at all"

"What is happening is absolutely alarming," says Pascal Schmid, the SVP's asylum spokesman. "Whoever crosses the border in Ceuta is, in fact, also in Switzerland," referring to the Schengen and Dublin areas, where there are no systematic internal border controls to stop irregular migration. For this reason, he supports the request of the Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni to suspend the Schengen agreement with Spain.

Schmid demands immediate measures: "The Federal Council must introduce systematic border controls immediately, as the SVP has been demanding for a long time." An "illegal mass migration" like in 2015 must be avoided at all costs, and the population must be protected from further imported crime. The democentric National Councillor and former judge also criticizes the "out-of-this-world" ruling of the Spanish court and denounces increasing legalization in the field of migration: "Intrusive justice disempowers politics, reducing its scope for action more and more."

"They are not refugees at all," Schmid reiterates. In the last session, the National Council established that the Maghreb states – Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia – must be considered safe countries of origin. Tens of thousands of unfounded applications, he argues, turn asylum law into a farce: "Once here, it takes a huge effort to expel them."

"Young people need a perspective"

Marc Jost, a National Councillor for the Evangelical People's Party of Switzerland (EVP), also criticizes the current approach: "The events in Ceuta clearly show that the combination of fences and general financial aid does not bring stability." It has not reduced migratory pressure or offered prospects to people on the spot. He proposes a two-pillar model, inspired by Swiss migration partnerships.

Firstly, a binding readmission agreement is needed for people without a right of residence: "Only a reliable procedure can combat trafficking networks and ensure the rule of law," says Jost. Secondly, EU funds should not be used for general aid but for concrete projects that create job and training opportunities.

Implications for Switzerland

The events in Ceuta and Melilla have reopened the debate on border management and immigration in Switzerland. The need for systematic border controls has been raised by several political parties, including the SVP, which is calling for immediate measures to prevent "illegal mass migration."

Implications for cross-border commuters

For cross-border commuters, the situation in Ceuta and Melilla could have direct implications. The reintroduction of asylum in embassies could make it easier for those in emergency situations to apply for asylum. However, systematic border controls could make it more difficult to cross between the two countries.
