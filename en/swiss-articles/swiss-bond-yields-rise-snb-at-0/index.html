Swiss bond yields rise, SNB at 0%

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 7 October 2026

I titoli della Confederazione a dieci anni rendono 0,58%

Context

TL;DR

  • The SNB keeps the policy rate at 0%
  • The yield has risen from 0,18% to 0,69%
  • Inflation is at 1%, between 0% and 2%

The 0,58% yield on ten-year Swiss Confederation bonds is the central figure in the movement observed in Switzerland. Although the SNB is keeping its policy rate at 0%, the yield paid to those financing the state is now significantly higher than in the recent past.

After hitting the annual low of 0,18% at the beginning of the war in Iran, the yield on the so-called «Eidgenossen» rose to 0,69% at the end of September, the high for 2026, and now stands at 0,58%.

Why the ten-year maturity reacts

Swiss yields follow global dynamics, albeit in attenuated form. They are driven above all by developments in international bond markets: the SNB sets the cost of money in the short term, but does not directly determine the yield on ten-year bonds.

Longer-term bonds are affected by expectations for future monetary policy, inflation, growth, the state of public finances and demand for bonds.

«The Swiss bond market is not completely immune to international interest-rate movements, but it reacts in a much more muted way than the major foreign markets,» Markus Thöny, an expert at Lombard Odier Investment Managers, explains to the Awp news agency.

In September, inflation in the Confederation reached 1%, the highest level in the past two years, driven mainly by energy costs; the figure nevertheless remained within the SNB's target range, between 0% and 2%. The franc also has a special role: when uncertainty rises, demand for this currency may increase. The appreciation of the franc helps contain prices, but penalizes exports.

Switzerland also benefits from relatively sound public finances. Therefore, unlike the United States, France and Japan, the rise in yields does not signal growing concerns about debt sustainability. In the United States, the size of the deficits, the large volume of government bonds on offer and uncertainty over inflation and growth are weighing; in Europe, spending needs for infrastructure and defense weigh, while in Japan the central bank has begun normalization after decades of ultra-expansionary monetary policy.

Operational details

The practical meaning for those following Switzerland

The ten-year figure should not be read as an automatic reflection of the policy rate. For those who live or work in Switzerland, the distinction helps explain why bond yields can move even when the SNB keeps its short-term decision unchanged.

Separating the signals

SNB policy rateThe price of short-term money

The first comparison to make therefore concerns the nature of the movement. A higher yield on ten-year bonds does not, by itself, mean that the SNB has raised the policy rate. The source links the rise mainly to international bond markets and describes the Swiss reaction as muted.

The second key concerns public finances. The rise is not presented as the result of growing concern about the sustainability of Swiss debt. This element distinguishes the Confederation from the picture evoked for the United States, France and Japan and makes it possible to read the figure without automatically turning it into a negative assessment of the state.

For the cost of living, the reference to follow is inflation: in September it rose again to 1%, mainly because of higher energy costs, but remains within the SNB's target range, between 0% and 2%. The costo della vita in Svizzera can be observed alongside this variable, without confusing it with the ten-year yield.

The franc introduces another angle of interpretation. During periods of uncertainty, demand for the currency may increase; the strength of the franc tends to curb prices, but weighs on the export economy. Monitoring cambio valuta is therefore consistent with the role attributed by the source to the national currency.

The overall picture remains linked to growth, inflation, demand for bonds and public finances. These are the factors to keep separate when assessing the Swiss movement, rather than looking for a single explanation in the SNB rate alone.

Key points

From the news to personal monitoring

The yield on Confederation bonds can be followed through a reading sequence built on the data and factors indicated by the source. The aim is to distinguish the observed value, its maturity and the forces driving it.

Four operational steps

1. Identify the object: the data concern ten-year Confederation bonds, the so-called «Eidgenossen». It should not be replaced with the SNB policy rate, which concerns short-term money.

2. Reconstruct the sequence: note the annual low of 0,18%, the high of 0,69% reached at the end of September and the current value of 0,58%. The sequence shows the yield’s path without confusing the three moments.

3. Separate monetary policy and the market: check, on the one hand, the SNB policy rate, at 0%, and, on the other, expectations about future monetary policy, inflation, growth, public finances and demand for bonds.

4. Add the Swiss signals: check inflation, which rose to 1% in September due to higher energy costs, and the role of the franc during periods of uncertainty. The strength of the franc tends to contain prices, but weighs on the export economy.

This framework avoids two overly quick readings. The first is to attribute every change in bonds to an SNB decision. The second is to interpret the rise as evidence of concerns about debt sustainability, whereas the source describes Swiss public finances as relatively solid.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the current yield on ten-year Swiss Confederation bonds?
According to the source, the yield on ten-year Confederation bonds is currently 0,58%. This figure represents an increase from the annual low of 0,18% recorded at the start of the year and is approaching the high of 0,69% reached at the end of September. The SNB policy rate remains unchanged at 0%.
Why can the yield on ten-year bonds rise while the SNB policy rate remains at 0%?
The yield on long-term bonds is not determined directly by the SNB policy rate, which concerns short-term money. According to the article, longer maturities are influenced by expectations about future monetary policy, inflation, growth, public finances and demand for bonds, as well as movements in international bond markets, which explain the rise observed despite the unchanged SNB rate.
How does Swiss inflation compare with the SNB’s target, and what does it indicate about the cost of living?
Inflation in Switzerland rose back to 1% in September, thus remaining within the BNS target range of between 0% and 2%. This level indicates moderate price pressures, mainly linked to higher energy costs, and can be used as a reference to monitor the trend in the cost of living in the country.
