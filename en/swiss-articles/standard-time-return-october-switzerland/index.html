The return to standard time

LUGANO – In the night between Saturday 24 October and Sunday 25 October, at three in the morning, the clocks will be set back one hour. The transition will mark the return from daylight saving time to Central European Time, also referred to as standard time or normal time. It will be light earlier in the morning, while darkness will arrive earlier in the afternoon.

The most immediate result will be an extra hour of sleep for those who can take advantage of it. The same change, however, will mean one less hour of light in the evening. The source describes this very dual effect as the starting point for the new controversies: the time change continues to divide opinion, and many people are calling for its abolition.

The fact that people can sleep longer on Sunday has not made either the adoption of daylight saving time in spring or the return to standard time in autumn popular. A recent survey by the Forsa institute indicates that 71% of respondents consider the time change unnecessary and would like it abolished. This percentage reflects the respondents' views; it does not indicate that abolition has already been decided.

The text presents two categories of objections. Some people point to health consequences, particularly sleep disorders. Critics also question whether moving the clock hands achieves its original objective, namely energy savings. The discussion therefore starts from a concrete, date-specific operation but broadens to its effects on rest and the difficulty of demonstrating the energy benefit. Calls for abolition are reported as part of the debate, not as an outcome already formalized in the text.

Operational details

The comparison between light and consumption

The original idea behind the time change

The reason cited by the source is the original goal of saving energy. Introducing daylight saving time in spring was supposed to reduce electricity consumption during the brighter months. The intended mechanism was straightforward: by moving the hands forward one hour, natural light would be used more and reliance on artificial lighting would be reduced.

The return in autumn makes the other side of the calendar visible. With the hands moved back, morning becomes light earlier, but darkness arrives earlier in the afternoon. Sunday offers an extra hour of sleep, while the evening loses an hour of light. The source thus presents the time change as a trade-off between different periods of daylight, not as a change limited to the clock face.

Key points

The sleep perspective

Light as a regulator of the rhythm

In the debate, numerous sleep specialists argue for maintaining standard time, or winter time, because switching from one time schedule to another could alter the rhythm and duration of rest. According to this position, standard time better respects the balance between sleep and wakefulness.

Among the elements that regulate the biological clock and the sleep-wake rhythm, the source points to natural light, with particular reference to the blue component of sunlight. The experts cited believe that winter time preserves this balance better; the switch to daylight saving time, on the other hand, is associated with a possible lack of sleep.

The consequences indicated in the text are declines in concentration and performance, together with an increased risk of accidents. These effects are presented within the framework of the sleep specialists' arguments. However, the source does not provide a quantification for the individual episode, nor does it measure the extent of the disturbances or any possible increase in risk.

What remains open

The cited survey offers an indication of respondents' orientation, while the analyses on energy and the specialists' observations concern different aspects. These elements alone do not establish either a measure of the health damage or the absence of any savings. Likewise, the difficulty of demonstrating the energy benefits does not erase the objective for which daylight saving time was introduced.

The picture presented is therefore that of a custom still in force, challenged because of possible sleep disturbances and energy benefits that are difficult to prove. The source describes the return to standard time, calls for abolition, and the positions of critics, but does not report a decision already adopted to eliminate the time change. In the text, the certain fact remains the moving of the hands of the clock at three and the switch to Central European Time.

When exactly does the switch to standard time take place?
The change takes place during the night between Saturday, October 24 and Sunday, October 25: at 3:00, the clock hands are turned back one hour, marking the return to Central European Time (standard time).
How many Swiss people would like to abolish the time change?
According to a survey by the Forsa institute cited by the source, 71% of respondents consider the time change unnecessary and are calling for its abolition. The figure measures the expressed opinion, not a decision that has already been made.
What are the main criticisms of the time change?
