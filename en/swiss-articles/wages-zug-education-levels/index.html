Wages and work in the Canton of Zug: training and levels (cross-border guide)

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 23 August 2026

Swiss labour market: salaries by degree of training, legal hours, compulsory social security (AVS, LPP, LAMal) and collective agreements.

Context

In brief

  • No federal minimum wage; some cantons set their own
  • Training (AFC, maturity, university) determines salary level
  • Maximum working hours: 45-50 hours/week by federal law
  • Mandatory pension: AVS 5.3%, LPP 7-18%, LAMal for all

Key facts

  • What: Salary structure based on training and collective contracts
  • Where: Switzerland, including Canton of Zug
  • Who: Employees with professional training (AFC) or higher
  • Working hours: Maximum 45 hours/week (industry, offices, commerce) or 50 hours (other sectors)
  • Pension: AVS 5.3% + LPP 7-18% by age + LAMal mandatory

The Swiss labor market

Switzerland has no federal minimum wage. Wages are determined mainly by three factors: training level, sector, and collective bargaining. In the Canton of Zug, as in other Swiss cantons, this dynamic regulates salary structures and career opportunities for employees.

The professional training (Federal Certificate of Capacity - AFC) represents the entry-level for many sectors: manufacturing, construction, hospitality, retail, craftsmanship. Higher education diplomas (maturity or professional, higher diploma courses) and university degrees (Bachelor and Master) open access to positions with significantly higher wages.

The maximum working hours are set by federal laws: 45 hours per week in industries, offices, and commerce; 50 hours in other sectors. Minimum holidays are four weeks per year (five for workers under 20). Maternity leave covers 14 weeks at 80% of regular pay through Loss of Earnings Indemnities (LEI); paternity leave is two weeks. Operational details

Social contributions deducted from salary

A significant part of the Swiss remuneration consists of mandatory social contributions, deducted from the gross salary. Understanding this structure is essential to evaluate the real value of a job offer.

Old-age and Disability Insurance (AVS/AI): Represents the first pillar of the pension system. The employee contributes 5.3% of the salary; the employer contributes equally, for a total of 10.6%. This finances the Swiss Social Security Pension Fund, which provides old-age (AVS), disability (AI), and maternity/paternity benefits (IPG) pensions.

Unemployment (AD) and Insolvency Indemnity (AC): The employee contributes 1.1% of the salary up to a yearly maximum established and indexed annually. These funds protect the worker in case of involuntary job loss.

Accident Insurance (LAA): Varies from 0.7% to 1.5% of the salary depending on the industry. The employer pays almost the entire contribution.

Full Pension Fund (LPP/BVG): For salaries above a coordination threshold (annually indexed), 7–18% of the coordinated salary is paid based on age. A 25–34-year-old contributes 7%; a 35–44-year-old contributes 10%; a 45–54-year-old contributes 15%; a 55-year-old and above contributes 18% until retirement age. These contributions finance the complementary pension (second pillar) through company or collective pension funds.

Health Insurance (LAMal): Is mandatory for every resident in Switzerland and is privately contracted with insurance companies. It is not a tax or a salary contribution, but a monthly fixed expense. Premiums vary by canton and region; adult deductibles range from CHF 300 to CHF 2,500 per year. The canton of Zug offers subsidies for premiums based on family income. Key points

How to Verify and Negotiate Your Salary

Step 1: Research Salary Benchmark

Before job interviews, gather data on what professionals with your profile (education, experience, sector, canton) earn at similar companies. Access UST/BFS and SECO. The more information you gather, the stronger your negotiation position will be.

Step 2: Check the Applicable Collective Agreement

If you work in a sector covered by a collective agreement (banking, metalworking, chemistry, tourism, healthcare, construction), your employer is bound by the minimum salaries established. Contact the trade union association in your sector to obtain the CCL document and salary thresholds for your level of experience.

Step 3: Negotiate at the First Interview

The first job interview is the ideal time for clarifications on remuneration. Present your salary benchmark research, highlighting education, experience, and added value. Propose a salary range (not a fixed amount), and remain flexible on alternative benefits: home office, continuous training, performance bonuses, additional insurance.

Step 4: Check Contributions on Your Pay Slip

Once hired, examine your first pay slip:

  • AVS/AI/IPG contributions: 5.3% of gross income
  • Pension fund (LPP): active if salary exceeds coordination threshold
  • Health insurance (LAMal): registered with a Swiss insurer
  • Unemployment contributions (AD): 1.1% up to maximum
  • Accident insurance (LAA): 0.7–1.5% depending on sector

If you discover irregularities, contact your employer or trade union immediately.

Step 5: Plan Your 3a Pillar

Swiss pension system is based on three pillars: (1) AVS (federal basic pension), (2) LPP/BVG (company or collective pension fund), (3) 3a Pillar (individual savings with tax deductions).

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the minimum wage in Switzerland and the Canton of Zug?
There is no minimum wage at the Swiss federal level. Remuneration is determined by collective bargaining by sector, training and experience of the worker. Some cantons have their own minimum wage (e.g. Geneva, Neuchâtel, Valais, Jura), but most have not, including the Canton of Zug. Workers must negotiate according to their profile and the collective agreement of their sector.
How much does training (AFC, diploma, degree) affect salary?
Strongly. According to UST/BFS, a worker with AFC earns significantly less on average than a technician with a higher degree, which in turn earns less than a university graduate. The average difference between an AFC and a Bachelor is about 50-70%. A Master's degree typically entails an even higher salary. These disparities remain stable in the years following first employment.
What are the social contributions (AVS, LPP, LAMal) deducted from the salary?
AVS/AI/IPG: 5.3% by employee. Unemployment (AD): 1.1% up to the annual ceiling. Accident insurance (LAA): 0.7–1.5% second sector (almost always paid by the employer). Pension fund (LPP): 7–18% by age, on salaries above the coordination threshold. LAMal: private expenditure with monthly premiums per canton, deductibles from CHF 300 to CHF 2,500 per year. Together they guarantee health and social security protection to all residents.
How can I check if my salary is competitive?
Consult UST/BFS and SECO to compare average salaries in your industry, canton and education level. Check if your industry has a collective agreement (contact the union). If your compensation is below the contractual benchmark or minimum, you can negotiate with the employer or consult a workers' association to defend your rights.
Can I contribute to Pillar 3a while working in Switzerland?
Yes, anyone with income from work can pay to the third pillar 3a with tax deductions. The annual ceiling is indexed every year. Contributing is optional but recommended to reduce the federal, cantonal and municipal taxable income, accumulating private savings until retirement age. Check our calculator to plan your payments.
