Federal Council without women: Rita Fuhrer’s analysis

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 5 October 2026

Rita Fuhrer, former Zurich State Councillor and former UDC candidate in 2000, comments on the succession in government and the issue of the presence of women.

Context

TL;DR

  • Rita Fuhrer is 73 years old and a former member of Zurich’s cantonal government.
  • In 2000, the Swiss People’s Party designated her as a candidate for the Federal Council.
  • Samuel Schmid was chosen, not Fuhrer or Roland Eberle.
  • Fuhrer does not consider a woman necessary to succeed Parmelin.

Key facts

Person → Rita Fuhrer; Age → 73 years; Candidacy → Swiss People’s Party, 2000; Parliamentary contest → more votes than Roland Eberle; Elected → Samuel Schmid; Cantonal office → Zurich government, 1995-2010; Interview source → SonntagsZeitung; Position → no problem with a Federal Council without women.

In 2000, the Swiss People’s Party officially nominated Rita Fuhrer as a candidate for the Federal Council. At 73, the former member of Zurich’s cantonal government is still the only woman the party has ever nominated for the national executive. In the interview published by SonntagsZeitung and reported by Keystone-ATS, Fuhrer argues that Guy Parmelin’s successor need not necessarily be a woman.

“Those days are over: it simply has to be the person best suited,” says the Zurich politician. The statement sums up her position: in her view, the decisive criterion should be the person’s suitability, not the fact that she is a woman.

The 2000 precedent

Fuhrer then reconstructs the vote that kept her out of government. She says she received more votes from Parliament than Roland Eberle, the second official candidate. In the end, however, neither of them prevailed; Samuel Schmid did.

According to Fuhrer, she had received a great deal of support within her party and among the bourgeois parties, right up to the centre of the Christian Democratic People’s Party. She attributes the failure to achieve final success mainly to the Social Democratic Party and the Greens. She adds that this was compounded by a non-partisan Bernese lobby supporting Schmid: the people of Bern wanted to bring one of their own back into the executive.

Asked about the left’s vote, Fuhrer assumes that her stance was considered too closely aligned with the Swiss People’s Party. She says she maintained a proper tone and, in principle, defended centre-right values. During hearings with the other parties, moreover, she did not make promises consistent with their political orientation. For the left, at least at that time, a candidate who was not too rigid in representing their own party would have been more important than electing a woman.

The politician finally recalls the demands of family life. Many women active in Parliament and in executive bodies have children, she observes, while the role of Federal Councillor leaves little time for family on most weekdays. In her case, her husband took care of many family responsibilities and supported her. The conclusion remains clear: a Federal Council without women would not bother her either; perhaps later there could be a female majority. Fuhrer says she never wanted to obtain a position solely because she was a woman.

Operational details

The practical significance of the affair

The first useful distinction for reading the case is between candidacy, political support and election. In Fuhrer’s account, these three moments do not coincide: the UDC’s official designation opened the race for her, the parliamentary contest gave her more votes than Eberle, but the final result favored Schmid. For the reader, this fact does not authorize treating the votes obtained by a candidate as an automatic ranking. Instead, it shows that, in this affair, the choice can be read together with the alliances and support evoked by the former State Councillor.

The second distinction concerns attribution. Fuhrer identifies PS and Verdi as forces that, in her view, prevented her from reaching the Federal Council; she also speaks of the Bernese lobby that allegedly supported Schmid. These are explanations offered by the protagonist, not a new parliamentary outcome reported in the source. Writing that those parties alone determined the decision would go beyond the text. The correct formulation is therefore to report her account as such.

Woman, party and family

The passage on the presence of women does not amount to an automatic call for a female candidacy. Fuhrer does not believe that Parmelin’s succession should be entrusted to a woman simply because she is a woman. Rather, she says that the most suitable person should prevail and that it would not be satisfactory to obtain office solely because of gender. At the same time, her experience shows that personal choice is linked to family circumstances: the role of Federal Councillor means, in her view, barely seeing one’s family on most weekdays, while support from one’s spouse is not always possible. This is a personal assessment, not a rule presented for all women in the Cantons or in Parliament.

The national significance of the news therefore lies in the way the composition of the Federal Council is discussed: suitability criteria, political affiliation, support among parties and the weight of cantonal backgrounds. The text does not, however, announce a legislative change, a deadline or a direct effect on family budgets. It contains no amounts concerning costo della vita in Svizzera, nor does it indicate a decision on the succession. For this reason too, it is advisable not to turn a political interview into an economic measure.

To use this news item correctly, the first step is to mark the attribution: this is an interview by Rita Fuhrer published by SonntagsZeitung and reported by Keystone-SDA. It is not presented as a decision of Parliament. This distinction prevents turning an individual opinion into a formal SVP/UDC position or an institutional outcome.

The second step is to separate dates and time periods. The reference to the year 2000 concerns the candidacy recalled by Fuhrer; the 1995-2010 period concerns her past in the Zurich cantonal government. Guy Parmelin's succession, on the other hand, is the political theme of the interview. Who is Rita Fuhrer and what is her connection to the Federal Council?

What is Rita Fuhrer's position on the presence of women in the Federal Council?
Rita Fuhrer argues that the succession of Guy Parmelin should not necessarily lead to the appointment of a woman. According to Zurich politics, the times when a gender criterion was imposed are over and the most suitable person must simply be chosen. She also stated that a Federal Council without women would not create problems for her and that she never wanted to get a role just because she was a woman.
Why was Rita Fuhrer not elected to the Federal Council in 2000?
According to Rita Fuhrer's reconstruction, despite having received a lot of support in her party and in the bourgeois forces up to the center of the PDP, the final failure was mainly due to the opposition of the PS and the Greens, who probably considered her line too close to the UDC and would have preferred a less rigid candidate. To this would be added a non-partisan Bernese lobby that favored the election of Samuel Schmid to bring a Bernese representative back into the executive.
