Neutrality, Cassis: we are neutral and will remain so

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 28 September 2026

Ignazio Cassis says he is satisfied after the initiative was rejected: Switzerland can continue with the current practice and assess economic sanctions on a case-by-case basis.

When commenting on the rejection of the initiative on neutrality, Federal Councillor Ignazio Cassis clarified that the vote was not against neutrality. The Ticino native said he was pleased that the definition of the principle had not been tightened and that current practice could continue.

«We were neutral yesterday, we are neutral today and we will also be neutral in the future», the Ticino native emphasized.

The first vote as the person responsible for the dossier

Although he has sat on the executive since 2017, this was Cassis's first vote experienced in his role as the person responsible for a dossier. This circumstance is reported alongside his satisfaction with the outcome: for the federal councillor, the possibility of continuing with current practice remains open. In his view, the definition of the principle has not been tightened.

A neutrality adapted to the times

Cassis described Swiss neutrality as a principle adjusted according to the period: more restrictive at certain times, more open at others. He recalled that it preserves the country's security and independence. He also clarified that being neutral does not mean being alone or indifferent.

The federal councillor then welcomed the possibility for Switzerland to continue deciding on a case-by-case basis whether to apply economic sanctions against other countries, always taking its own interests into account. The wording used by Cassis therefore refers to assessing each individual situation.

The wording reiterates the two elements cited by the foreign minister: neutrality protects security and independence, but does not take a single form in every period. Finally, Cassis reiterated the importance of the principle for Swiss history and identity. In his view, the debate that developed over these months demonstrated this.

Operational details

For those who live or work in Switzerland, the practical reading of the vote lies in the difference between the definition of neutrality and the way the principle is applied. Cassis presents the result as confirmation of a margin: the principle has not been made more rigid, while practice can continue to adapt to the period.

Neutrality does not mean isolation

Cassis has ruled out a reductive reading of the concept. Being neutral does not amount, in his explanation, to being alone or indifferent. The principle retains a link with the country's security and independence, but its configuration can be more restrictive or more open depending on the period. This dual indication helps read the result without turning it into a promise of behavior that is always the same.

The same approach avoids confusing the debate over Swiss history and identity with the individual economic application. The identity-related weight of neutrality, invoked by Cassis and demonstrated by the debate that developed over these months, does not eliminate the case-by-case criterion for sanctions. In his reading, the two levels remain connected: the principle preserves security and independence, while practice adapts to the circumstances.

The practical consequence for the reader is therefore a criterion for interpretation, not a new requirement: when faced with a decision on sanctions, one must separate the existence of the principle from the assessment of the individual measure. Key points

Three steps for reading the result

1. Establish the outcome. The initiative on neutrality was rejected, and Cassis did not interpret the vote as a rejection of neutrality. 2. Separate definition and practice. His reading distinguishes the principle, which has remained non-rigid, from the possibility of continuing with current practice. 3. Check the economic criterion. When sanctions against other countries are discussed, the reference is to a case-by-case decision, assessed by looking at Switzerland's interests.

This framework helps avoid two opposite simplifications: reading the rejection as an abandonment of neutrality or considering the principle a rigid formula. Cassis's words instead support both dimensions: neutrality remains linked to security and independence, but its application may be more restrictive or more open depending on the period.

The third step is the one with the most concrete implications for those who follow Swiss public life. It is not enough to ask whether the country is neutral; one must look at how the individual decision on economic sanctions is assessed. The source links this choice to the country's interests and does not offer an identical formula for every situation. For this reason, the most correct procedure is to read the general principle, current practice and the specific economic decision separately.

This sequence makes it possible to keep the issue within its actual scope. The vote concerns the definition of neutrality and the continuity of the practice described by the federal councillor; the economic reference concerns the possibility of applying sanctions against other countries.
