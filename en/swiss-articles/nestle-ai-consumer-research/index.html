  • When: Tuesday during an investor conference

  • Monitoring: Daily access to language models

On Tuesday, Anna Manz, Nestlé's chief financial officer, revealed a changing business strategy at an investor conference in Boston. The group monitors on a daily basis how language models access its websites. “We monitor every day to what extent big language models interrogate our websites instead of people, and this is increasing day by day,” Manz said. This monitoring is not a niche activity, but a central operational priority. The numbers speak for themselves: AI bot access is constantly growing, transforming traffic to corporate sites.

Operational details

AI as the New Product Discovery Filter

Nestlé's move illuminates a broader economic trend: artificial intelligence is becoming the primary filter through which consumers discover information and products. It is not merely a technological change, but a reallocation of visibility power. When a consumer asked Google for 'best protein snack', the results were organized according to ranking algorithms. When the same consumer asks ChatGPT the same question, they receive a narrative response generated by the model. If Nestlé is not mentioned in that response, its invisibility is total. If it is recognized as a source, the effect is even more powerful than a simple ranking, because authority is attributed to it.

This explains why the daily monitoring that Manz describes is not a matter of corporate vanity, but of calculated business strategy. The company has understood that control over its own narrative in AI results has become as critical as control over its homepage. The growth of AI bot access to Nestlé's sites, described as constant and daily, testifies to the acceleration of this phenomenon. It is not temporary; it is structural.

For Swiss companies operating in marketing, communication, and brand management, the message is implicit. Ignoring the phenomenon means risking becoming invisible in AI-generated results, regardless of product quality or company history. Nestlé, with its accumulated heritage advantage, is well positioned to capitalize on this transition. Smaller companies that do not adopt similar strategies risk falling behind.

Heritage and Accessibility: The Competitive Formula

What Manz describes as an advantage — 160 years of history and years of research — is not rhetoric, but a concrete quantifiable resource. Language models are trained on vast corpora of public texts. If Nestlé makes its research data, certifications, scientific studies, and documentation easily accessible (through technical structures such as sitemaps, rich metadata, APIs), the probability of being cited as a source by models increases measurably.

It is an evolved form of SEO, applied not to traditional search engines, but to generative artificial intelligence. And the phenomenon is not a niche. Millions of people ask questions daily to chatbots; visibility in AI responses represents an unprecedented brand awareness multiplier in the era of communication.

Key points

How Swiss companies need to adapt concretely

For those working in marketing, communication and brand management in Switzerland, Nestlé's message is imperative: adaptation to AI is not optional, it is urgent. A website that is well optimized for Google is not enough; you need to ensure that your information assets are accessible and citable by current and future language models.

Concretely, the first step is to identify relevant product or service categories and analyze how consumers ask AI questions about these topics. Nestlé works with external partners to do this; smaller companies can start by directly testing public chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude and others). The critical question is simple: when a consumer asks a language model for a recommendation in your industry, is your company mentioned? If not, the next step is to understand why.

The second step is to structure the business data so that bots can access and understand it. This means:

  • Publish studies, white papers and research in an open and easily citable format
  • Structure website metadata (schema.org, Open Graph, JSON-LD) for bots to understand context
  • Monitor traffic coming from language models, exactly as Nestlé does on a daily basis
  • Collaborate with AI and advanced SEO experts to optimize visibility into the results generated by the models

Frequently Asked Questions
Why does Nestlé monitor the access of language models to its sites every day?
Nestlé monitors AI bot traffic because the number of logins is increasing daily. Understanding how and how much AI accesses your sites is critical to adapting your marketing strategy. If language models account for an increasing fraction of total traffic, your content needs to be optimized for them, not just human consumers.
What are the 'prompts' that Nestlé analyses?
Prompts are the questions that consumers ask directly to AI. Nestlé works with external partners to analyze these prompts in relevant product categories. For example, when a consumer asks ChatGPT 'what is the best protein supplement', that question is one that Nestlé wants to understand to ensure it is quoted in the generated answer.
What is the ultimate goal of Nestlé's artificial intelligence strategy?
The goal is not just to appear in AI-generated responses, but to be recognized and cited as a trusted source by language models. Nestlé intends to make its 160-year heritage of history and multi-year research accessible to models, so that it is considered an authoritative authority on topics relevant to its business.
How can other Swiss companies adopt a strategy similar to Nestlé's?
Swiss companies can start by directly testing public language models (ChatGPT, Claude) to see if they are mentioned in the answers. Then, they need to analyze how consumers ask questions in relation to their products and ensure that their data is structured and accessible to bots. Collaborating with partners who specialize in AI and marketing speeds up the process.
