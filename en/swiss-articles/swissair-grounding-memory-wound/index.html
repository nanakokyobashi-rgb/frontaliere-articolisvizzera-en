The day of the grounding

On October 2, 2001, after 4:00 p.m., the Swissair grounding turned a working day into a rupture for the entire staff. Sandrine Nikolic-Fuss, today president of the Kapers cabin crew union and a former company employee, was not on duty: she was six months pregnant and followed the event from the ground.

Before the grounding, warning signs had emerged, but when everything came to a standstill, reality seemed completely unreal to her. The aircraft remained on the ground, passengers were stranded somewhere in the world, and the crews did not know how they would continue working.

For Nikolic-Fuss, the collapse affected more than just one company. With Swissair, a national symbol in which Switzerland had long recognized itself sank. The employees lost what she calls their professional home, a loss that could not simply be digested. Even years later, the trade unionist could not shake the feeling that Switzerland had left her alone. Those who had lived through that day had seen how quickly something considered unshakable could be destroyed.

At the decisive moment, neither personal commitment nor years of loyalty managed to protect the employees. For Nikolic-Fuss, it was a watershed that shaped her work as a trade unionist. Integration into the Lufthansa Group may have saved Swiss aviation economically, but it did not erase the lesson of the grounding: economic success and the strength of a brand are not enough on their own to guarantee secure jobs and reliable conditions.

Since 2001, she adds, the industry has changed profoundly. External crises, strong cost pressure, competition, working conditions and contracts have entered a phase of increasing pressure. The trade unionist does not claim that everything was better back then; she does, however, recall a stronger awareness of the value of trained and experienced staff. At Swissair, people did not simply feel employed, but part of a company, a history and a special professional culture.

Operational details

The work the brand does not tell

From the uniform to the responsibilities

The most concrete part of the testimony concerns what happens inside the cabin. From the outside, the work of flight attendants is often reduced to onboard service. Nikolic-Fuss instead highlights evacuations, firefighting, first aid and critical decisions. This distinction changes how a job is viewed: the company’s image does not encompass the responsibility entrusted to the staff.

The third dimension is the team. In confined spaces, one must remain calm, be flexible and rely on one another. From this emerge a particular solidarity and a strong team spirit: elements that do not erase fatigue or uncertainty, but explain why the profession left such a profound mark on the unionist’s life.

The comparison between then and now should not, however, be reduced to nostalgia for Swissair. Nikolic-Fuss admits that not everything was better back then. The point is another: at the company, the staff felt part of a company, a history and a special professional culture; today, before choosing, the same person would reflect longer on hours, health, family, salary and contract. For those who live and work in Switzerland and look toward aviation, the practical criterion therefore becomes measuring professional identity and the reliability of the conditions together.

The profession nevertheless retains a value that the testimony does not conceal: encounters with different cultures, international teams and direct experience of the world. The balance is therefore not between an easy job and a difficult one, but between strong personal appeal and conditions that call for a clear-eyed assessment.

Key points

A checklist for making an informed decision

1. Start with the actual duties

Anyone considering a cabin crew job can start with a simple question: does the role include only passenger service, or also evacuations, firefighting, first aid and critical decisions? The answer defines the professional importance of the position and prevents one from stopping at the job’s outward image. When consulting the annunci di lavoro, this distinction should remain central to the reading.

2. Assess daily organization

The second step is to lay out irregular hours, the impact on health and work-family balance. These are not separate items: together they describe how much the profession can affect personal life. The assessment must also include salary progression and contractual conditions, identified by the union representative among the factors not to be underestimated. Noting these elements before choosing helps compare enthusiasm for flying with the reality of the job.

3. Assess training and the team

The source highlights the value of trained and experienced staff and the qualities needed on board: calm, flexibility and mutual trust in confined spaces. For this reason, the choice concerns more than just the brand. One must ask whether one’s way of working is compatible with critical responsibilities, international teams and solidarity built during service.

4. Balance motivation and prudence

Nikolic-Fuss’s experience shows that the profession can broaden horizons through encounters with people from different cultures, work in international groups and direct experience of the world. But the same experience leads one to reflect longer before making the same choice again. The final criterion can be put this way: the desire to fly must hold up even after considering health, family, salary and contract. It is a concrete method for deciding without relying solely on the power of a brand.
