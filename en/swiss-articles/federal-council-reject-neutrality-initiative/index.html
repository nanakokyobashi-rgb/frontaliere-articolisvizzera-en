Neutrality: Federal Council denies constitutional tightening

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 11 August 2026

The federal government is calling for the neutrality initiative to be rejected, warning that a rigid definition would limit Switzerland's room for manoeuvre.

Context

In a nutshell

  • The government calls for rejecting the neutrality initiative.
  • Current practice is considered protective for Swiss interests.
  • The text would reduce the room for manoeuvre in international relations.

Key facts

  • What: Recommendation to vote on the neutrality initiative.
  • Who: Federal Council.
  • Promoters: Pro Switzerland and members of the UDC.
  • Campaign launch date: 4 August.
  • Objective: Prevent the inclusion of a rigid definition in the Constitution.

The Federal Council has officially expressed its position on the popular initiative on neutrality, calling on the people and cantons to reject the proposed text. According to the executive, current practice has proved itself, ensuring effective protection of the interests of the Confederation. The government believes that a stricter constitutional definition, such as the one advocated by the promoters, would end up overly constraining Swiss foreign policy, drastically reducing the room for manoeuvre needed to manage global geopolitical challenges. The initiative, promoted by Pro Switzerland together with some representatives of the UDC, aims to include a precise definition of neutrality in the Federal Constitution. Operational details

The analysis of the practical implications reveals a significant divergence between the vision of the promoters and that of the federal executive. On the one hand, the initiative seeks to crystallize the concept of neutrality into a rigid constitutional norm, eliminating the grey areas that today allow the Federal Council to act with a certain diplomatic discretion. On the other hand, the government argues that such rigidity would result in dangerous isolation, preventing Switzerland from reacting promptly to violations of international law through instruments such as economic sanctions. A comparative analysis between current practice and the proposed amendment shows how the management of foreign policy would be profoundly transformed. Currently, Switzerland can decide to align itself with international sanctions even in the absence of a UN mandate, if it deems it appropriate for its own interests or for the protection of international law. If the initiative is approved, this capacity for action would be revoked, limiting the Confederation to a passive role. This change would directly impact the country's economic stability, as sanctions are often used as diplomatic and trade leverage. The debate also extends to the defence sector: the initiative would categorically exclude membership in alliances, an issue that already raises intense debates in Parliament. Frequently Asked Questions
What exactly does the neutrality initiative ask for?
The initiative, promoted by Pro Switzerland and members of the UDC, requires the inclusion in the Constitution of a rigid definition of neutrality. This provides for the prohibition of joining military or defence alliances, except in the event of a direct attack against Swiss territory. In addition, it requires the Confederation not to adopt sanctions against belligerent States, unless they have been decided by the UN.
Why is the Federal Council opposed to this initiative?
The Federal Council considers that the current practice of neutrality is effective and adequately protects Swiss interests. According to the executive, a rigid constitutional definition would drastically limit the country's room for manoeuvre, preventing a flexible management of foreign policy and reducing the ability to react to violations of international law.
What would be the consequences in case of approval of the initiative?
If approved, Switzerland would lose the flexibility to impose sanctions autonomously if not authorized by the UN, as happened in the case of Russia. The government warns that this would harm the country's interests and limit options for defence cooperation, which is essential for a small state.
