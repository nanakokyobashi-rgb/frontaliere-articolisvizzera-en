Artificial intelligence: impact on human rights

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 7 October 2026

## TL;DR - 63rd session of the Human Rights Council in Geneva - Switzerland led a coalition on transitional justice - Privacy

Key facts

From Bern, the communiqué published on October 7, 2026, reports the conclusion, in Geneva, of the 63rd session of the Human Rights Council. The discussions examined artificial intelligence, the risks it poses to human rights and the opportunities it offers to strengthen respect for them.

In the report, Switzerland is active on two fronts: it committed to resolutions on the impact of AI and led a group of States on transitional justice. The first strand also concerns services explicitly cited by the source, such as human resources management and access to housing.

AI between equality and disinformation

Switzerland supported two resolutions devoted to the consequences of AI for gender equality, non-discrimination and combating disinformation. Their aim is to curb the circulation of deepfakes and content related to online harassment, phenomena whose spread has been facilitated by new technologies.

The communiqué links these risks to the use of AI in services. It does not limit the issue to the digital sphere in the abstract: it cites human resources management and access to housing. New technologies must be designed in a way that ensures respect for the right to privacy and the principle of non-discrimination.

Transitional justice and international issues

A second result concerns transitional justice. A coalition of States led by Switzerland enabled the adoption of two resolutions. The four pillars identified are establishing the truth, combating impunity, providing reparation and guaranteeing non-recurrence of violations.

According to the communiqué, examining the past serves to address tensions and prevent violence from resuming. After a war, transitional justice is described as a tool for maintaining peace. New technologies can help document violations and search for missing persons.

During discussions on specific situations, Switzerland supported extending the mandate of the United Nations Special Rapporteur monitoring the human rights situation in Russia, reiterated its condemnation of indiscriminate drone attacks against civilians, denounced sexual violence used as torture against civilians and prisoners of war in Ukraine and supported extending the mandate of the mission tasked with investigating crimes committed during the conflict in Sudan. Regarding reform of the architecture of the UN human rights system, its appeal to harmonize the operating methods of treaty bodies received the support of 86 States.

Operational details

The practical meaning for work and home

The most concrete connection with the lives of those who live or work in Switzerland lies in the examples chosen in the statement: human resources management and access to housing. The source does not describe an individual outcome or a procedure to follow; instead, it indicates an approach: when AI is used in services, its design must respect the right to privacy and the principle of non-discrimination.

For work, the practical question is whether the use of AI in human resources management is assessed through these two principles. For housing, the same lens concerns access to the service. The source stops at this approach and does not detail an individual outcome. The passage remains relevant because the statement calls for new technologies to be designed in a way that guarantees specific rights.

The table separates the realm of services from that of transitional justice. In the former, the reader focuses on privacy and non-discrimination; in the latter, on the possibilities offered by new technologies for documenting violations and searching for missing persons. Confusing the two realms would lead to reading every use of AI as if it had the same purpose.

Disinformation and content related to online harassment add another question: does the problem concern the service or the spread of content? The two resolutions on AI referenced by Switzerland address precisely this set of risks.

Key points

From the news to an orderly verification

The communiqué allows for a concrete reading procedure, but not an administrative request. It does not indicate a form, an office or an operational deadline for anyone living or working in Switzerland. To remain faithful to the source, the action therefore consists in classifying the area, applying the principles referred to and distinguishing the different uses of AI, without attributing to the resolutions effects that the text does not describe.

Four steps based on the source

4. Recognize transitional justice. If the dossier concerns documenting violations or searching for people who disappeared after a war, consider the four pillars indicated: reconstructing the truth, combating impunity, reparation and ensuring that violations do not recur.

The scope of the news item

The date of October 7, 2026 and the 63rd session concluded in Geneva define the scope of the communiqué. The text records Switzerland's position in the work of the Human Rights Council and in the process of reforming the structure of the UN system concerning human rights. It does not describe an individual procedure for challenging the use of AI, nor does it indicate a national deadline. This distinction prevents an international position from being turned into an obligation that the source does not announce.
