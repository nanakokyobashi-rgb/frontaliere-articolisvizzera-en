A historic turning point for the orange giant

Zurich is witnessing a potential paradigm shift in the structure of one of Switzerland's largest companies. According to reports by 'NZZ am Sonntag', Migros is evaluating a deep restructuring that could lead to the merger of its ten regional cooperatives. The most radical hypothesis, which CEO Mario Irminger would prefer, is the creation of a single cooperative for the entire country. However, no definitive decision has been made yet, although an agreement between the parties is reportedly not far off.

The current organizational structure dates back to 1941, when founder Gottlieb Duttweiler consciously chose to create strong regions and a weaker central body. This model, which has remained unchanged for over 80 years, is today considered obsolete by many industry observers. The reasons for the possible reform are operational in nature: the current system involves duplicated structures and lengthy decision-making processes, which slow down the company's responsiveness in an increasingly competitive market.

The issue is not purely administrative but touches the core of Swiss cooperative identity. Migros responded to press inquiries by confirming ongoing work, referring to its 'Vision 2035' strategy. This vision aims for greater efficiency and a simpler organization to ensure long-term competitiveness. The options on the table

According to the Sunday edition, several variants are being studied to resolve the inefficiencies of the current model. One hypothesis would involve reducing the cooperatives from ten to three, divided by geographic zone: one for western Switzerland, one for central Switzerland, and one for eastern Switzerland. This solution would maintain a degree of decentralization but drastically reduce bureaucratic complexity. Another possibility, preferred by Irminger, is a total merger into a single national entity. This second option is the most drastic and would entail significant changes in the company's governance.

The weight of the referendum and the members

The reorganization proposed by Migros is not a simple administrative act, but a complex legal process that directly involves Swiss citizens. To abolish or merge a cooperative, a referendum is generally necessary, requiring a two-thirds majority for approval. This means that approximately 2.3 million Migros members could be called upon to participate in the final decision. This is a huge number, representing a significant portion of the Swiss population and employees.

Member participation therefore becomes crucial. Every member has the right and duty to inform themselves about the consequences of such a decision. A reform without a referendum, although this possibility is being evaluated according to information from the 'NZZ am Sonntag', would be a delicate and potentially controversial step. The members' trust in the transparency of the process will be decisive. If members do not feel represented or informed, the risk of opposition or boycott is real. For those who are members, it is important to monitor the company's official communications to understand how the consultation will take place, if planned.

Implications for the labor market and costs

A merger of this magnitude is not limited to governance: it has direct repercussions on employees and the Swiss labor market. The elimination of structures currently present in multiple entities could lead to a greater reduction in jobs. In a context where the cost of living in Switzerland is already high, every change in the labor market has a tangible impact on wages and job stability. Migros employees, as well as suppliers and business partners, will have to adapt to a new organizational reality.

From an economic point of view, the search for efficiency aims to ensure long-term competitiveness. This could translate into more stable prices or greater operational efficiency, which directly affects consumers. However, the risk of downsizing in the administrative and logistics sectors is a fact to be considered. Swiss companies operate in a regulated market where transparency and human resources management are under the scrutiny of SECO and cantonal authorities. What to do while waiting for decisions

Since there is no final decision yet, the current phase is one of observation and preparation. For Migros members, the most important thing is to stay updated. Migros has not denied the ongoing work, which indicates that the process is underway but not yet formalized. Members should keep an eye on official company communications, which could include invitations to meetings or technical documentation on the merger proposal. In the event of a referendum, participation is fundamental: the two-thirds majority makes every vote decisive. Ignoring the consultation could mean losing the opportunity to influence the structure of a company that employs thousands of people in Switzerland.

For employees and workers in the sector, it is time to evaluate their position within the company. A restructuring can lead to new opportunities, but also to reorganizations that require specific skills. It is advisable to update your CV and evaluate your career options in a job market that, while solid, is constantly evolving. Swiss companies tend to invest in the training of their employees, but during a merger, priorities can change, shifting towards immediate efficiency.
