Made in Europe: Switzerland seeks place in EU procurement

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 25 September 2026

On 24 September 2026 in Brussels, the inclusion of Switzerland in the EU industrial acceleration law was discussed, with Germany in favour and France against.

On 24 September 2026, in Brussels, the possibility of including Switzerland was discussed in the European discussion on the future "Made in Europe" initiative. German Economics Minister Katherina Reiche called for including trading partners such as Switzerland; French Industry Minister Sébastien Martin claims that public funds should only go to industries in the Member States of the European Union.

The discussion took place upon the arrival of the two ministers at the Competitiveness Council, a meeting attended by the relevant ministers of the 27 EU member states. On the table is the strengthening of European industry. The concept of "Made in Europe" is part of the law on industrial acceleration proposed by the European Commission in the spring.

European preference in procurement

The proposal aims to introduce a 'European preference' in public procurement in certain industrial sectors. The point that is still controversial is the definition of "European": it remains to be established whether and to what extent third-party countries such as Switzerland can fall within the framework.

Reiche spoke about «Made with Europe» and referred to production in Europe together with trading partners, mentioning Norway, Switzerland and Canada. Martin replied that in “Made in Europe” there is “Europe”, referring to the 27 EU states. He specified that the proposal does not include duties and that the priority is an agreement between the 27; Switzerland, he added, does not sit in the Council.

Poland and Spain also left several possibilities open. Polish State Secretary for Economic Affairs Michal Baranowski said it was too early to say whether Switzerland would be included, pointing to the involvement of key partners and different rules for product categories, as long as they were simple. Spanish Industry Minister Jordi Hereu first indicated the group of 27, then a possible expanded group with the EEA states and consolidated partners, which could include Switzerland. A third group would include countries with free trade agreements or other specific agreements.

Swiss business associations and the Confederation are following the negotiations closely. In early September, a high-level economic delegation traveled to Brussels.

Operational details

The practical point, for Switzerland, is not yet a new applicable rule, but the criterion with which the EU could recognize an external partner in accessing community industrial benefits. The source describes an open negotiation and does not indicate a final decision. For the Swiss economic associations and the Confederation, the question to follow is therefore what position will end up in the law on industrial acceleration.

Four scenarios on the table

The first scenario is the German one: European production could include that made with commercial partners, with an explicit reference to Switzerland. In this approach, the fact of being a third country would not automatically close the door to preference in public procurement of the sectors concerned.

The second is the French one. Martin links the word "Europe" to the 27 EU states and wants to direct public funds to the industries of the member states. For a Swiss reality, this line would leave political discussion open, but does not offer the inclusion advocated by Germany. The source specifies, however, that the proposal does not include duties: the comparison concerns the destination of public funds.

The third scenario is more flexible. Poland wants to involve key partners and evaluates different rules based on product categories. The constraint indicated by Baranowski is operational: the rules should remain simple and not add bureaucracy to businesses. It is a criterion that, for those observing the Swiss position, is as important as the choice of group to which one belongs.

Spain proposes a group structure: first the 27, then the EEA states and consolidated partners, with Switzerland among the possible names, and finally the countries linked to the EU by free trade agreements or other specific agreements. Here too, the Swiss inclusion is not defined.

The national data already available is the level of institutional attention: The Confederation and Swiss economic associations are following the discussions closely, while a high-level economic delegation went to Brussels at the beginning of September. The distinction avoids transforming a European negotiation into an already decided consequence for the Swiss market.

The reader can therefore use three indicators: who is included, whether the rules change by sector and whether the commitment remains not to increase bureaucratic burdens. These are the concrete elements highlighted by the positions of Germany, France, Poland and Spain.
