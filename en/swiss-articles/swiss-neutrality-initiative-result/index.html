Neutrality initiative: rejected by 70,2% of voters

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 28 September 2026

70,2% of voters rejected the initiative on neutrality. The no vote came from all Cantons, with voter turnout at 47,1%.

70,2% of the Swiss electorate rejected the Neutrality Initiative today. All cantons voted no, and voter turnout stood at 47,1%, according to the Keystone-ATS projection. The proposal, promoted by Pro Svizzera and several UDC figures, had been opposed by the other major political groupings.

The text, officially entitled «Safeguarding Swiss neutrality», called for a permanent and armed definition of neutrality to be enshrined in the Constitution. It provided that Bern would not join any military or defense alliance, except in the event of a direct attack, and would renounce sanctions against belligerent states, except those decided by the UN. For skeptics, including the Federal Council, a stricter definition would have excessively limited foreign policy’s room for maneuver and harmed the country’s security.

A vote with no grey area

The polls had left the initiative’s backers little room for hope, but the scale of the defeat was judged a debacle. Political scientist Urs Bieri, of the gfs.bern institute, attributed the result to the UDC’s difficulty in mobilizing voters beyond its own base. He also noted that the support of former Federal Councillor Christoph Blocher, including financially, had not been enough: to win, in his view, coalitions and an issue perceived as urgent are needed.

Ticino rejected the initiative with 51,7%, differing only partly from the national result. Apart from the Italian-speaking canton, only Schwyz, at 57,9%, and Appenzell Innerrhoden, at 59,7%, remained below 60%. In 12 cantons, the rejection exceeded 70%: the highest figure was Basel-Stadt, at 79,9%, ahead of Neuchâtel at 75,6% and Zurich at 74,5%. That group also included Basel-Landschaft, Bern, Vaud, Solothurn, Lucerne, Jura, Zug, Fribourg and Aargau. In Graubünden, the no vote reached 66,6%; in the city of Bern, it reached 88,9%. This time, there was no trace of the Röstigraben.

The vote comes amid an unfavorable run for proposals supported by Switzerland’s largest party. In March, 61,9% had rejected reducing the radio-TV licence fee to 200 francs; in June, 54,8% had rejected the initiative against a Switzerland of 10 million.

Operational details

From the vote to Swiss interests

The source places the weight of the consultation on three key points: the Federal Council's room for maneuver in foreign policy, sanctions, and Switzerland's credibility as a mediator. For those who live or work in the country, the practical connection indicated by the debate therefore passes through security, international relations, and business interests.

The two economic interpretations

The promoters argued that the principle of neutrality, enshrined back in 1848, had been hollowed out of its meaning and shaped to move closer to NATO and the EU. In their view, protecting this cornerstone of Swiss tradition meant defending Switzerland's freedom and prosperity. Distance from power blocs was also presented as an economic advantage: companies would be able to maintain business relations with anyone.

To this approach, the 'no' camp countered with the value of flexibility. The Federal Council and other skeptics argued that a more rigid constitutional definition would excessively reduce the room for maneuver in foreign policy, with potential harm to the country's security. The same objection applied to sanctions: according to the opponents, the initiative's constraints went beyond current practice and risked harming Switzerland's interests and reputation.

Frequently Asked Questions
What was the national outcome of the vote?
The Swiss electorate rejected the initiative called "Safeguarding Swiss Neutrality" with a majority of 70.2%. The rejection was expressed in all the cantons, with an overall turnout of 47.1%. The proposal, supported by Pro Switzerland and by representatives of the UDC, aimed to include a rigid definition of armed neutrality in the Constitution, limiting military alliances and international sanctions.
How did Ticino rate compared to the rest of Switzerland?
Ticino rejected the initiative with 51.7% of the votes against. Although it has deviated from the national result, the Italian-speaking canton is among those with the lowest percentage of rejection, along with Svitto (57.9%) and Appenzello Interno (59.7%). In contrast, in 12 Cantons the no exceeded 70%, reaching peaks such as 79.9% in Basel City and 88.9% in the city of Bern.
What were the main arguments of the opponents?
The Federal Council and other opponents argued that a rigid constitutional definition would excessively reduce the room for manoeuvre of Swiss foreign policy. According to this vision, flexibility is fundamental to the security and reputation of the country. Moreover, the limitation to sanctions approved by the UN alone was deemed detrimental to Swiss interests, as it would have limited the ability to act in complex international contexts.
