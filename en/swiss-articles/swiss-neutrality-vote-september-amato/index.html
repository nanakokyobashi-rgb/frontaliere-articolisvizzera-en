An issue concerning the country’s future

Ahead of the September 27 federal vote on the Popular Initiative for Neutrality, supporters of the initiative are sometimes accused of playing into Russian propaganda or of seeking to undermine relations with the West. Corrado Amato rejects this interpretation and calls it a label designed to sidestep the real crux of the debate: “What kind of Switzerland do we want to leave for future generations?”

The fundamental question, the author writes, is simple. Switzerland must continue to be a neutral, independent, and sovereign country, capable of autonomously determining its own foreign and security policy. The point, he adds, is not to choose between Moscow, Washington, or Brussels, but to choose Switzerland: “It is not a matter of being pro-Russian, pro-American, or pro-European. It’s a matter of being pro-Swiss.”

Operational details

What’s Really at Stake on September 27

The popular initiative on neutrality, which will be put to a vote on September 27, touches on one of the historic pillars of Swiss foreign policy. For those who live and work in Switzerland—residents, employees, cross-border workers, and businesses—the issue is not an abstract one, because neutrality shapes the way the Confederation participates in international sanctions, peacekeeping missions, and multilateral economic agreements.

The position expressed in this article is based on a firm conviction: renouncing neutrality would mean subjecting fundamental decisions—on security, foreign policy, and the allocation of resources—to supranational bodies over which Swiss citizens have limited influence. This concern is directly linked to the ongoing debate in Bern regarding the relationship between national sovereignty and European integration.

The Icelandic Precedent as a Point of Comparison

The Icelandic vote on August 29—52.8% “no” to reopening negotiations with the EU, with an 82.5% turnout—offers a point of comparison explicitly cited by the author. Reykjavík did not choose isolation, but rather democratic control over its own decisions. For Switzerland—a country that is not an EU member but maintains a relationship with Brussels based on a network of bilateral agreements—this parallel raises a question: To what extent is it possible to combine access to the single market with the preservation of decision-making autonomy?

The article also notes that the Federal Council itself recognizes the link between neutrality, security, and prosperity. This institutional recognition reinforces, in the author’s view, the idea that neutrality is not a relic of the past but a foreign policy tool that remains relevant today.

Key points

How to find your way ahead of the vote

For a citizen living in Switzerland, September 27 is a date to mark on the calendar. The Popular Initiative on neutrality will be decided at the ballot box: for those eligible to vote, it is time to learn about the text, its practical consequences, and the positions of the main political and economic players.

Corrado Amato's article is not a voting guide, but an opinion piece. However, it contains verifiable elements that are useful for orientation:

  • The Federal Council recognises that neutrality safeguards the country's security, independence, and prosperity.
  • The Icelandic vote of August 29 (52.8% 'no', turnout 82.5%) is cited as a significant precedent.
  • The debate fits into the broader relationship between national sovereignty and supranational bodies.

What to do before September 27

A concrete procedure ahead of a federal vote can be outlined in a few steps.

1. Check your registration on the electoral roll. Each Canton manages its own register: it is worth checking in good time that your details are correct, especially after a move or a change of municipality.

2. Read the text of the initiative. The text of the Popular Initiative on neutrality is published on the Confederation's portal and on the Cantons' websites. Reading it directly helps to distinguish between political debate and regulatory content.

3. Consult the official recommendations. The Federal Council and Parliament issue a voting recommendation, available in the federal messages and parliamentary committee reports.

4. Assess the economic implications. For those working in export-oriented sectors, in border Cantons, or in regulated industries, it is worth considering the potential impact on international relations and trade agreements.

Frequently Asked Questions
When do you vote in Switzerland on the People's Initiative for Neutrality?
The federal vote is scheduled for September 27, as indicated in the article by Corrado Amato. The author describes the text as a key step for the country's foreign and security policy, stressing that the consequences will fall mainly on the next generations.
Why does the author reject the accusation of Russian propaganda?
Corrado Amato defines the label as a rhetorical shortcut that prevents discussion on the merits. His summary is clear: "It is not a question of being pro-Russian, pro-American or pro-European. It's a matter of being pro-Swiss." For the author, the defence of neutrality concerns the right of Switzerland to decide for itself.
What does the Federal Council say about neutrality according to the article?
The author recalls a significant political fact: it is the Federal Council itself that recognizes that neutrality protects the security, independence and prosperity of the country. An institutional recognition that, in the thesis of the article, makes it difficult to justify a renunciation of this position.
What precedent does the article cite to support the sovereignty thesis?
Amato brings the example of the Icelandic vote of 29 August: Icelanders rejected the proposal to reopen negotiations for membership of the European Union with 52.8% of the votes and an turnout of 82.5%. They would not choose isolation, but democratic control over their decisions.
Why does the vote on 27 September also concern citizens and businesses?
According to the article, neutrality conditions the way the Confederation transposes international sanctions, participates in European programmes on research, training and defence, and manages multilateral economic agreements. The repercussions, the author recalls, are measured at the cantonal and municipal levels.
