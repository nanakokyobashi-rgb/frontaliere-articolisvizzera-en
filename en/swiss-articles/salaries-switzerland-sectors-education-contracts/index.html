Gross, contributions, and net: composition of a Swiss salary

When an employee receives a Swiss payslip, they do not see the gross salary agreed upon in the contract directly. The transition from gross to net occurs through standardized and mandatory deductions applied monthly.

From each salary, contributions to AVS (old-age insurance), AI (disability insurance), and IPG (loss of earnings allowance) are deducted: the employee contributes a total of 5.3% of the gross salary. This is supplemented by unemployment contributions (AD/AC), amounting to 1.1% up to an annual maximum, and accident insurance contributions (LAINF/LAA), which range from 0.7% to 1.5% depending on the sector of activity.

The occupational pension fund (LPP/BVG) deducts a percentage of the coordinated salary that depends on the employee's age: 7% for those aged 25 to 34, 10% for those aged 35 to 44, 15% for those aged 45 to 54, and 18% for those aged 55 and above until the reference age. This increasing contribution reflects the greater accumulation of pension capital as one approaches retirement.

Operational details

Salaries by level of training and sectors of activity

The Swiss labour market significantly rewards the level of training achieved. Those with a compulsory school diploma (primary/lower secondary education) generally have access to operational positions in sectors such as manufacturing, construction or auxiliary services, with low salaries. A basic vocational training diploma (federal certificate of ability, AFC) or a second cycle secondary qualification opens access to technical, craft or administrative roles, with considerably higher salaries.

Holders of a tertiary diploma-both university (Bachelor/Master) and higher professional (diplomas of university vocational schools, SUPs) -position themselves at the top of the salary distribution, accessing coordination, technical specialization, research or management positions. This wage differential by level of training is structural in the Swiss market.

The sector to which they belong also has a significant impact on wages. Manufacturing, finance, and insurance tend to offer high wages thanks to productivity and operating margins. Retail, hospitality and ancillary services have lower average salaries. Agriculture shows a broad spectrum, but often with lower wages than the advanced tertiary sectors.

Key points

How to read a Swiss payslip and check your salary

A Swiss payslip transparently shows: the gross contractual salary, the detailed breakdown of all contributions (AVS/AI/IPG, AD/AC, LAINF/LAA, LPP), the total gross amount from which the contributions are deducted, and finally the net amount paid into the worker's bank account. For Swiss residents, the document also specifies the federal withholding tax, while the cantonal and communal withholdings are calculated based on the annual tax return.

To check if your salary is in line with the market, it is useful to consult the data provided by UST/BFS, which publishes monthly and annual statistics on the salary structure by sector, profession, region, and level of education. If you are classified in a sector covered by a CCT, your specific collective agreement sets the minimum wages for each category: check that your gross amount is not lower than what is provided in the salary table for your professional category and years of seniority.

Official tools and sources to consult data on salaries and employment

To verify certified information on salaries, employment, and the labor market, refer to these main tools: SECO (State Secretariat for Economic Affairs) provides a comprehensive database of collective agreements, employment statistics, and sectoral benchmarks. UST/BFS (Federal Statistical Office) publishes the Salary Structure Survey (ISS), with regional data disaggregated by sector, profession, and level of education. Cantonal administrations provide specific information on cantonal minimum wage (where applicable), LAMal subsidies, and local tax rates. Trade unions and employers' associations allow direct consultation of the specific CCT for your sector and profession.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the average salary in Switzerland?
UST/BFS publishes monthly data on the structure of wages with average and median values disaggregated by sector, profession, degree of training and region. The median gross salary varies greatly from CHF 4000 to CHF 8000+ per month depending on these factors. For data specific to your sector and canton, please consult the official UST/BFS statistical portal.
How much is being withheld from my gross salary?
Compulsory social deductions (AVS/AI/IPG 5.3%, AD/AC 1.1%, LAINF/LAA 0.7–1.5%, LPP 7–18% according to age) amount on average to 25–35% of the gross. To these are added federal, cantonal and municipal taxes, which vary from 10% to 20% of the gross depending on the canton and tax situation. Use the [calcolatore](nav:calculator) for a custom estimate.
Does my canton have a minimum wage?
There is no Swiss federal minimum wage. Some cantons (e.g. Geneva, Neuchâtel, Jura, Basel-City) have introduced cantonal minimum wages with specific amounts, while most cantons have not established a minimum. Check with the cantonal administration of your canton of residence or consult SECO.
What are collective agreements and do they concern me?
Collective Labour Agreements (CLAs) are negotiated agreements between trade unions and employers to set minimum wages, hours, holidays and working conditions in a specific sector. If your employer adheres to a CCL, you are entitled to the benefits provided by the salary table of your category. Check the CCL of your sector at your union or through SECO.
How do I calculate my net salary?
Starting from the gross contractual salary, deduct social contributions (about 25–35% of the gross) and cantonal taxes (about 10–20%, variable by canton and income), taking into account age, marital status and other tax factors.
