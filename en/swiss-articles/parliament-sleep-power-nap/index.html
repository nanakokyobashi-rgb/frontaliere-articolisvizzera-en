Parliament needs a place to sleep: power nap

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 29 September 2026

During sessions, Swiss parliamentarians sleep an average of 6 hours and 10 minutes, about 50 minutes less than usual, according to Björn Rasch's study of 16 members of Parliament.

Context

In brief

  • Parliamentarians sleep 6 hours and 10 minutes per night
  • Rasch's study concerns 16 parliamentarians
  • The proposal is to create spaces for short naps
  • Fatigue can make decisions riskier

Key facts

  • Sample → 16 members of the National Council and the Council of States
  • Sleep during sessions → 6 hours and 10 minutes per night
  • Difference → approximately 50 minutes less than usual
  • Proposal → spaces for short naps at the Federal Palace
  • Researcher → Björn Rasch, professor at the University of Fribourg
  • Reported effects → riskier decisions and difficulty assimilating information
  • Reference → Blick

Six hours and ten minutes of sleep per night is the average recorded among Swiss parliamentarians during sessions. The figure is at the center of a study conducted by Björn Rasch, a sleep researcher and professor at the University of Fribourg, involving 16 members of the National Council and the Council of States. The study, reported today by Blick, indicates approximately 50 minutes less rest than usual.

For Rasch, the reduction is not merely a number related to schedules. Tired people, the researcher argues, tend to make riskier decisions and have greater difficulty assimilating information. Hence the connection between quality of rest and parliamentary work: a session may take place with representatives present, but fatigue can affect how they assess information and choices.

The proposal at the Federal Palace

The proposal is to create spaces at the Federal Palace for short naps during the day, the so-called power nap. Rasch does not suggest sleeping in the chamber as a solution. He explains it with a question:

«If a parliamentarian fell asleep in the chamber, it would immediately be a scandal; leaving them sitting there, awake but tired, is not a solution: why not create spaces for recovery?»

In his view, a room dedicated to rest could help maintain the parliamentarians' efficiency. The idea therefore concerns a brief, organized recovery in the Parliament's spaces.

The researcher ultimately broadens the topic beyond parliamentary life. He hopes that sleep will receive greater attention in the national strategy for salute, because sleep disorders can indicate stress levels and be associated with a greater risk of burnout in subsequent years. He also calls for more space for the topic in schools, especially for children and adolescents. The source thus presents a specific proposal for the Federal Palace and, at the same time, a broader request for greater attention to sleep.

Operational details

What the proposal really measures

The practical value of the survey lies first and foremost in its scope. The data concerns 16 members of the National Council and the Council of States, observed during sessions. The point of comparison indicated by Rasch is their usual rest: during those periods, they sleep about 50 minutes less than usual. This distinction makes it possible to read the result without turning it into a general measure of the sleep of all people living in Switzerland.

For those who follow parliamentary work, the concrete question is not merely whether a representative remains awake in the chamber. It is whether fatigue can affect the assessment of information and the level of risk of decisions, as Rasch argues. The opportunity for a brief nap is presented precisely as a possibility for recovery during the day, intended to address that problem before fatigue affects efficiency.

Duration and quality of rest

The source does, however, highlight that getting little sleep is not the only factor to consider. Sessions are often accompanied by aperitifs at which alcoholic drinks are present. Alcohol, the expert explains, can make it easier to fall asleep, but in the second half of the night it promotes more frequent and prolonged awakenings. The result can therefore be less restorative sleep, even when falling asleep seems easier.

A similar line of reasoning applies to sleeping pills. They help people fall asleep, but do not guarantee restorative rest. The survey shows that only a minority of parliamentarians use them, in line with the general population. The power nap, alcohol and sleeping pills are therefore not presented as equivalent: the first is a daytime recovery break, while the other two elements concern the quality of nighttime rest.

The reference to salute broadens the discussion beyond the Federal Palace. Rasch links sleep disorders to the level of stress and a higher risk of burnout in subsequent years, while calling for greater attention in schools to children and adolescents. The national angle of the proposal therefore lies in the relationship between rest conditions, parliamentary decisions and attention to health strategy, not in the introduction of a new rule already described in the source.

Key points

How to follow the topic in a concrete way

To turn the news into an orderly verification, the reader can follow a precise sequence without confusing the proposal with a decision already made.

Four useful steps

  • Separate evidence and proposal. The evidence is the study conducted on 16 members of the National Council and the Council of States; the proposal is to create spaces for short naps at the Federal Palace.
  • Correctly attribute the effects. The source reports that, according to Rasch, tired people tend to make riskier decisions and have more difficulty assimilating information. This is the researcher's assessment linked to the study data.
  • Examine sleep quality. It is necessary to distinguish the duration of rest from the elements described in the source: alcohol can help one fall asleep but favor awakenings in the second half of the night; sleeping pills do not ensure restorative rest.
  • Follow the two paths indicated by Rasch. The first concerns recovery spaces for parliamentarians; the second concerns greater attention to sleep in the national health strategy and in schools, especially for children and adolescents.

This reading procedure avoids two errors. The first is presenting power naps as a measure already implemented in Parliament. The second is turning the reference to sleeping pills into a recommendation: the source describes their use as a minority practice and does not propose doses, therapies or medical instructions.

For those following federal institutions, the point to check in any developments is therefore the distinction between a proposal by Rasch and an actual measure. The news provides data on sleep during sessions, indicates possible consequences for decisions and raises the issue of the health strategy and schools. These are the three elements to keep separate when assessing the continuation of the debate.

How much do parliamentarians sleep on average during sessions?

What effects on decision-making does Rasch attribute to lack of sleep?
Rasch argues that fatigue leads to making riskier decisions and makes it more difficult to assimilate information, as demonstrated by the same study on parliamentarians.
What proposal has been put forward to improve parliamentarians' rest?
