The debate and the reserves

On Wednesday, in Burgdorf, the discussion also involved the people responsible for the mandatory reserves. The meeting served to describe the concrete situation, the challenges and the possible measures while Parliament examines the initiative «Yes to secure medical supply» together with the government counterproposal.

The starting point is the shortage of medicines, which has concerned Switzerland for years. The examples cited include antibiotics and painkillers. The warehouse described during the meeting stores millions of boxes of medicines; the facility occupies an area equivalent to five football fields and reaches 15 meters in height. A limited fraction of these packages constitutes the so-called mandatory reserves, that is, reserves intended to address situations of severe shortage.

Bernhard Megert, director of Alloga, explained the distinction between the packages available for the market and those subject to Confederation control:

«Of 10’000 packages of a medicine present in the warehouse, 3’000 constitute the mandatory reserve: the other 7’000 can be freely placed on the market, while authorization from the Confederation is required for those reserved».

The example shows that the physical presence of medicines in storage does not mean that all packages have the same status. The portion not included in the mandatory reserve can be freely placed on the market; federal authorization is required for the restricted share.

In recent years, recourse to this authorization has become more frequent. In 2018, the mandatory reserves were released on 17 occasions; from 2022 onward, the threshold of 100 annual releases was exceeded every year. These figures, presented in Burgdorf, describe the growing pressure on the reserve system.

For Peter Huber, the increase is attributable to the pandemic and armed conflicts. Huber chairs Helvecura, the cooperative responsible for coordinating the establishment of compulsory stocks. In his view, however, the reserves have always been sufficient. The data and this assessment therefore refer to two distinct aspects: the number of authorizations to release stocks and the adequacy of the available reserves.

Key points

Dependence on Asia for older medicines

The other issue concerns older medicines, those whose patents have expired. Urs B. Kientsch, a militia officer with the country's economic supply organization, identifies dependence on Asia as a separate issue from financing the reserves. Active ingredient manufacturers are concentrated there. The text therefore identifies the area where production is concentrated, but does not quantify the share of active ingredients involved.

The solution indicated by Kientsch is to encourage active ingredient manufacturers to return to Europe in order to supply the local market. For this to happen, in his assessment, policymakers must create favorable framework conditions. The source does not specify which conditions should be introduced, nor does it indicate any measure already decided or a timeline for the manufacturers' return.

The report also distinguishes between drug shortages and the availability of reserves: shortages have concerned Switzerland for years, while Huber states that the reserves have always been sufficient. The increase in authorizations is not presented as evidence that the stocks have been depleted. Rather, the documented discussion concerns how to finance them and dependence on foreign countries for certain medicines.

On the political level, Parliament is discussing the “Yes to Security of Medical Supply” initiative and the government's counterproposal. The Confederation says it is working on a solution for financing, but the text does not report a final decision. Both the economic issue of the reserves and the industrial issue linked to active ingredients concentrated in Asia therefore remain unresolved.
