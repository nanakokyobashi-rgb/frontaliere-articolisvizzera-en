Swiss labour market and the role of the cantons

The Swiss labour market is based on a federal structure that combines uniform national regulations with specific cantonal regulations. In the canton of Zug, as in the other cantons, there are common guidelines on the maximum duration of working hours, the right to holidays and compulsory contributions to federal social security (AVS/AI). However, wage strategy and employment conditions remain determined by local factors, sector demand and the level of training achieved.

Operational details

Salary levels by level of training

The Swiss wage structure strongly reflects the degree of training achieved. Data on the national labour market show significant differences between those who have only completed compulsory schooling, those who have a vocational training diploma (apprenticeship/AFC), and those who have a tertiary qualification (university, vocational university school, master's degree). In the Canton of Zug, the demand for skills is particularly high in sectors such as finance, insurance, information technology, construction, healthcare and logistics.

Salaries vary considerably between levels of training. An apprentice in training receives a lower remuneration, while a professional with a certificate of continuous training or with a university degree accesses significantly higher positions and salaries. Collective bargaining agreements and minimum wage

At the federal level, Switzerland does not have a uniform minimum wage. However, many sectors are regulated by collective labour agreements (CLAs), negotiated between trade unions and employers' associations, which set binding wage minimums. How to navigate the Swiss job market

For those looking for a job in the canton of Zug or planning to move for occupational reasons, it's helpful to understand the main tools and channels for searching. Job offers are published on federal platforms, cantonal portals, and sector-specific websites. The regional employment office, managed by the SECO at the federal level with operational offices at the cantonal level, offers free consultation and support in occupational search processes.

Before negotiating a work contract, it's advisable to:

1. Verify the sector: identify if your sector is covered by a collective contract. If so, the employer is bound by the CCL minimum wages, which generally offer additional protections and benefits (annual bonuses, loyalty premiums, right to continuous training).

2. Calculate the net salary: the Swiss salary slip is complex due to multiple deductions (AVS/AI, AD/AC, LAINF, IFD, cantonal and communal tax). Use a salary calculator to estimate how much you will actually receive. Results vary significantly depending on the municipality of residence.

3. Evaluate professional pension (LPP): check with the employer which pension plan is offered and what the contribution rate is. A generous LPP increases the real value of compensation in the long term and accelerates the accumulation for the pension.

4. Read the contract carefully: notice periods vary (1, 2, or 3 months depending on seniority). The non-compete clause, if present, can limit future job opportunities. Ensure that all verbal conditions are written down.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is there a cantonal minimum wage in the Canton of Zug?
No, the Canton of Zug has not established a uniform cantonal minimum wage. Switzerland, in general, does not have a federal minimum wage, leaving the cantons free to decide. However, many sectors are regulated by collective bargaining agreements (CLAs) that set binding wage minimums for the sector. In the absence of CCL, the salary conditions are determined by direct negotiations between employer and employee, always respecting the time limits (45–50 hours/week) and the right to 4 weeks of annua
How many working hours are mandatory per week in Switzerland?
The Federal Labour Law sets a maximum of 45 hours per week for the industrial, office and commercial sectors. For other industries, the limit is 50 hours per week. These are the legal ceilings; many employers offer lower hours (e.g., 42–44 hours). Excess hours may be compensated with equivalent time off or, in some cases, with an additional payment according to the collective agreement.
What contributions are withheld from my Swiss salary?
On a Swiss salary are withheld: (1) AVS/AI: 5.3% of the gross salary; (2) AD/AC: 1.1% up to the annual ceiling; (3) LAINF: 0.7–1.5% depending on the sector; (4) Direct federal tax (IFD), cantonal and municipal tax (vary by canton and municipality); (5) LAMal: health insurance, paid directly. The employer further pays 5.3% for AVS/AI and additional contributions.
How does occupational pension (LPP) work in Switzerland?
The LPP is mandatory for those with a coordinated salary above a threshold. Contributions vary by age group: 7% (25–34 years), 10% (35–44), 15% (45–54), 18% (55+ up to the reference age). The contribution is divided between employee and employer (normally 50/50 with variations). The LPP is added to AVS and covers a part of the pension in the event of retirement, disability or death.
What is maternity and paternity leave in Switzerland?
Maternity is guaranteed for 14 weeks at 80% of the last salary (covered by the federal Pregnancy and Maternity Allowance, IPG). Paternity is for 2 weeks with pay. Some cantons and employers offer longer periods or higher percentages. It is important to check the terms of the collective bargaining agreement or company agreement for potential surcharges beyond the federal minimum.
