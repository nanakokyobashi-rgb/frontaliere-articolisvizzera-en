Family reunification: bureaucracy blocks children for years

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 8 September 2026

A report by the Swiss Observatory denounces: slow procedures and difficult documents separate families up to the age of 10. Children pay the highest price.

Context

In Brief

  • Administrative procedures, not laws, are blocking family reunifications in Switzerland
  • Families examined remained separated for 9 to 10 years
  • Family reunifications in documented cases lasted up to 7 years
  • The Swiss Observatory demands a maximum duration of 1 year

Key Facts

  • Source: Swiss Observatory for Asylum and Foreign Law (SBAA)
  • Publication: Report published Tuesday
  • Average separation duration: Between 9 and 10 years in examined cases
  • Family reunification procedure duration: Up to 7 years
  • SBAA Proposal: Maximum 1 year for application review
  • Main impact: On children and minors

It is not the laws that are blocking family reunifications in Switzerland, but rather the slowness of administrative procedures. This is the claim made on Tuesday by the Swiss Observatory for Asylum and Foreign Law (SBAA) with a new specialist report that particularly points to the devastating consequences for children.

The problems are concrete: documents that are difficult to obtain, high costs, and constant shifts of jurisdiction between federal and cantonal authorities. These obstacles, apparently technical, transform into years of family separation. In the cases examined by the report, families remained separated for nine to ten years.

Administrative Obstacles

The main issue is obtaining the documents required by the authorities. In a specific case documented in the report, the authorities asked an Afghan man for an officially authenticated marriage certificate. To obtain it, the man was forced to return in person to Afghanistan, despite being in a dangerous situation. In another case involving a family of Eritrean refugees, an authority imposed the presentation of passports. To obtain them, the family members would have had to contact the authorities of the State from which they had fled, potentially putting their refugee status at risk. Only after a legal battle lasting two years was it recognized that this request was unacceptable.

Moreover, the procedures change depending on the competent authority. According to the report, the State Secretariat for Migration (SEM) applies less strict criteria than cantonal authorities. This difference becomes problematic when jurisdiction shifts: if a person obtains a residence permit, the file is transferred from the Confederation to the Canton and the application is re-examined with more stringent requirements.

Real Waiting Times

The family reunification procedures documented in the report lasted up to seven years. If you add the preceding asylum procedures and the waiting times provided by law, the separation can reach up to ten years: a substantial part of childhood that minors often spend abroad in precarious conditions and without secure residential status.

Operational details

The devastating impact on children

It is precisely children who suffer the most from procedural obstacles, the SBAA denounces in its report. During the years of waiting, minors find themselves separated from their parents in often precarious conditions, without legal certainty regarding their future in the country. The time of separation coincides with crucial years of their growth and development.

The situation worsens further when the dossiers pass from the federal to the cantonal authorities. The State Secretariat for Migration (SEM) applies different procedural standards than the cantons, and this creates a cascade effect: when responsibility passes from the Confederation to the canton, the dossier is subjected to a new examination with more stringent requirements. As a result, families who had gone through procedures at the SEM find themselves forced to start the process practically from scratch at the cantonal office.

The requests of the Swiss Observatory

Faced with this situation, the Swiss Observatory for the Right to Asylum and Foreigners has made concrete requests to the federal and cantonal authorities. The main proposal is to set the maximum duration for examining family reunification applications at one year, a term currently absent from the regulations.

In addition to the time limit, the SBAA requests that the authorities consistently take into account the special protection needs, especially of minors. This means not only speeding up times, but also adapting procedures to the vulnerable conditions of children. Finally, the report emphasizes that testing requirements should be tailored to the conditions of the country of origin and the protection status of the persons involved, avoiding imposing requirements that put the safety of applicants at risk.

Currently, requests for documents such as notarized certificates, passports or other official documents remain a major obstacle, especially when documentation needs to be obtained from states from which people have had to flee due to persecution.

Key points

How the reunification process works

Family reunification in Switzerland is a process that involves both federal and cantonal responsibilities. The State Secretariat for Migration (SEM) is the federal body that manages the first stages, but the dossier can pass to the cantonal authorities once the applicant obtains a residence permit. This transfer of competence, formally necessary for the Swiss administration, represents one of the critical points highlighted by the report.

Anyone who is in a situation of family separation and has well-founded reasons for reunification must prepare documentation demonstrating the family bond and the reasons why reunification is necessary. The documents required vary, but often include marriage certificates, birth certificates, and other documents that should be officially notarized. As emerges from the Observatory's report, this very phase of document collection is often the one that takes the longest, especially when the people involved come from countries where obtaining official certificates is complicated or dangerous.

What to do if you find yourself in this situation

If you are facing a family separation and considering submitting a reunification application, it is essential to carefully document all your communications with the authorities and response times. Keep copies of every document sent and every response received from the SEM or the competent cantonal authority.

Contact the SEM (State Secretariat for Migration) to understand which documents are specifically required in your case. When doing so, clearly indicate whether there are legitimate impediments to obtaining documents from certain countries. If your application is transferred to the cantonal authority, immediately check what additional requirements the canton may impose and ask for clarification to avoid further delays.

Frequently Asked Questions
How long does it currently take for a family reunification in Switzerland?
According to the Swiss Observatory report, documented family reunification procedures have lasted up to seven years. If previous asylum procedures and the legal waiting times are added together, separation can reach nine to ten years in the cases examined. The Observatory calls for this time to be reduced to a maximum of 1 year for the examination of applications.
What are the main obstacles in family reunification?
The report identifies three main obstacles: difficulties in obtaining notarized documents (such as marriage certificates), high costs of procedures, and transfers of responsibility between federal and cantonal authorities. A concrete example: an Afghan man had to personally return to Afghanistan for a marriage certificate; an Eritrean family faced a two-year legal battle because the authorities had asked them for passports from their authorities of origin, risking their refugee status.
How does the procedure change when it changes from the (federal) SEM to the cantonal authorities?
The State Secretariat for Migration (SEM) applies less stringent criteria than the cantonal authorities. When the application is transferred from the Confederation to the canton after obtaining a residence permit, the application is reviewed with stricter requirements. This means that families may find themselves restarting the process with different and more stringent standards.
What is the impact of delays on children?
Children suffer more from procedural obstacles, according to the Swiss Observatory. In documented cases, children spend up to ten years separated from their parents, often abroad in precarious conditions and without a secure residence status. These years coincide with crucial stages in their growth and development, negatively affecting their education, emotional stability and well-being.
What measures has the Swiss Observatory proposed?
The Centre has made three main proposals: to set the maximum duration for the examination of applications for family reunification at one year; to take into account in a consistent manner the particular protection needs, especially of minors; to adapt the proof requirements to the conditions of the country of origin and the protection status of the persons concerned, avoiding requests that put the safety of applicants at risk.
