Schengen Dublin Report: Impacts on GDP and Border (cross-border guide)

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 21 September 2026

The Federal Council adopted the report on Schengen and Dublin: abandonment could reduce Swiss GDP by up to 3.9% in 2035.

Context

In a nutshell

  • The Federal Council adopted the Schengen/Dublin report on 18 September 2026
  • Abandonment could reduce Swiss GDP by up to 3.9% in 2035
  • Estimated annual income loss up to CHF 1300 per capita
  • Relevant benefits for the economy, tourism, asylum and internal security

Key facts

Report date: 18 September 2026 Relevance: Swiss Federal Council Impact on GDP in 2035: Up to 3.9 percent Loss of income per capita: Up to CHF 1300 per year Reference postulate: Postulate 24.3946 of the Radical Liberal Group RL

On 18 September 2026, the Swiss Federal Council adopted an important report analysing the economic, financial and security effects of a possible abandonment of the Schengen and Dublin agreements with the European Union. Switzerland currently derives considerable benefits from these bilateral agreements, which ensure smooth cross-border movement for goods and people, key elements for preserving the well-being and attractiveness of the national economic centre. According to the external study that forms the basis of the official document drawn up on behalf of the National Council through the postulate of the Radical Liberal Group RL, leaving Schengen and Dublin would have extremely heavy consequences for the country within the time horizon of 2035.

The report shows that Switzerland's gross domestic product could shrink by as much as 3.9 percent in 2035.

Operational details

Analysis of impacts on key sectors and the labor market

A potential breakdown of the Schengen and Dublin agreements would cause profound disruptions and critical issues in the Swiss labor market, directly hitting the cross-border workforce and companies operating in Switzerland. Without effective cross-border free movement, estimates for 2035 indicate that cross-border workers could lose a total of 422,000 hours on working days due to massive traffic congestion generated by the new and inevitable customs controls at the border crossings. This slowdown in daily mobility would generate annual costs estimated between 1.08 and 2.27 billion francs for the Swiss economy. The reintroduction of controls would also result in a drop in the number of cross-border workers of up to 60 percent, triggering severe staff shortages especially in the economic sectors of catering and care, where foreign labor plays a structural and irreplaceable role.

Demographic and migratory dynamics

Repercussions on the labor market would not stop at the decrease in commuting workers. Due to daily transit difficulties and bureaucratic barriers at the border, the probability that up to 170,000 people will decide to permanently transfer their residence to Switzerland would increase drastically, significantly altering the demographic fabric and pressure on the residential real estate market of the border cantons. Key points

Internal security and judicial cooperation

Beyond purely economic and financial implications, the Federal Council report highlights the invaluable value of the Schengen and Dublin databases for safeguarding Switzerland's internal security. During 2025, solely thanks to the use of the Schengen Information System known as SIS, Swiss security authorities obtained nearly 40,000 relevant and targeted hits, especially regarding individuals flagged as dangerous or wanted for criminal activities. Switzerland would not have any autonomous or national measure capable of compensating for the loss of these shared databases and international police cooperation tools. The direct consequence would be the inability to timely or fully identify threats to public security, severely hindering the effectiveness of criminal prosecution and leaving the country exposed to high criminal risks.

Operational dependence and financial sustainability

Maintaining external borders and managing autonomous national databases would require colossal financial investments that could never be offset by savings resulting from a hypothetical withdrawal from the agreements. Furthermore, the effectiveness of border controls would depend to a large extent on the cooperation and human resources provided by neighboring countries and the European Union, effectively making Switzerland dependent on foreign decisions for the management of its territory and traffic flows. Customs queues would become all the longer and more paralyzing the fewer personnel were employed by neighboring countries at checkpoints.
