30'000 cubic metres of kerosene may be withdrawn from mandatory reserves. The country's Delegate for Economic Supply authorized its use on Thursday from October 15 to November 1. The quantity corresponds to 6,5% of what is available.

The intervention is linked to interruptions and considerable delays in international rail transport, especially from Germany. The reported risk is that Zurich Airport could run short of kerosene. The Confederation explains that trains are also failing to keep to schedule because large quantities of goods have been transferred to rail after river transport became impossible due to the low water level of the Rhine.

What is guaranteed and what is under pressure

The same communication specifies that Swiss supplies of petrol, diesel and heating oil have deteriorated compared with the previous week, but remain guaranteed. For people living in Switzerland, the wording therefore distinguishes between a deterioration in the situation and an interruption in availability: the note does not announce that these products have run out.

Asked about this, Zurich-Kloten Airport stated that, for now, departing aircraft are not subject to refuelling restrictions. UBAG manages the kerosene and deals with a total of nine external suppliers, including Socar, BP and Shell. All the fuel arrives at the airport by rail.

The airport management is not commenting on shortages at international level and refers to the airlines regarding refuelling flights bound for Frankfurt. Germany's main airport, which serves as a hub for Lufthansa, Swiss's parent company, is also experiencing difficulties. Lufthansa does not anticipate cancellations, but has invited crews bound for Frankfurt to depart with fuller tanks.

Operational details

The operational bottleneck is transport

The decision on reserves must be read together with one fact: all of the kerosene for Zurich-Kloten reaches the airport by rail. For this reason, disruptions to rail traffic directly affect the airport's fuel supply. The source links the difficulty to international delays, particularly from Germany, and to the diversion to rail of large quantities of goods that could no longer travel by river.

A proper reading avoids confusing three different elements: the availability of reserves, the punctuality of trains and the status of aircraft refuelling. The communication presents them in the same episode, but does not describe them in the same way.
