Russia interferes in Swiss neutrality vote (cross-border guide)

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 27 August 2026

RT.DE and social media used by Moscow to influence the September 27 neutrality vote. Government acknowledges threat of disinformation.

Context

In a nutshell

  • Vote on neutrality on September 27, 2026
  • Kremlin uses RT.DE and social media to influence campaign
  • Federal Government Recognizes Growing Threat of Foreign Disinformation

Key facts

  • What: Constitutional initiative on neutrality with prohibition of sanctions against states in armed conflict
  • When: Vote on September 27, 2026
  • Where: Switzerland
  • Who: Kremlin, media RT.DE, SEPOS, Federal Council
  • Opposition: Federal Council opposes the initiative
  • Source: SRF reports on the influence operation

The Kremlin has repeatedly tried to influence the campaign on Swiss neutrality in recent weeks, SRF reported. Swiss citizens will vote on the initiative on 27 September 2026. The proposal would write into the Swiss constitution a broad ban on sanctions against states involved in armed conflict, with the exception of Switzerland's obligations to the United Nations.

Moscow's campaign through the media

RT.DE, a Kremlin-funded newspaper, published several articles during the campaign. These articles were critical of the Federal Council, which opposes the initiative, and supportive of the proposal. Some of these articles are attributed to Hans-Ueli Läppli, although no journalist by this name can be identified. The surname is familiar in Switzerland from Alfred Rasser's fictional character, Theophil Läppli, a Swiss soldier

Operational details

# Expert analysis and normalization strategy

Ulrich Schmid, professor of Eastern European studies at the University of St. Gallen, explained the significance of the Kremlin campaign to SRF. The Kremlin is trying to present the war in Ukraine as an ordinary two-state conflict. Adding neutrality to the Swiss constitution would play into the hands of the Kremlin, normalizing the conflict. Schmid predicts that such influence efforts will likely have little direct effect on Swiss voters' choices. However, he stresses that Switzerland should still take them seriously, as attempts to influence Swiss public opinion are part of a much broader Kremlin strategy of destabilization. This strategy is designed to operate in the long term, according to the scholar's analysis.

# Federal Government Recognition and SEPOS Warning

The federal government recognizes the problem. In response to a request from SRF, the State Secretariat for Security Policy (SEPOS) stated that foreign disinformation and state-funded influence attempts, specifically targeting Switzerland, are on the rise. In a draft security policy document, SEPOS warns that foreign influence operations and disinformation can directly affect the results of elections and referendums. Such campaigns aim to polarise societies and undermine trust in democratic institutions.

Key points

The Federal Council's response and parliamentary measures

The Federal Council set up a working group at the end of 2025 to address foreign interference activities. The group aims to improve cooperation within the federal administration and increase public awareness of attempts at foreign interference. However, Parliament believes that more needs to be done. Both Houses are considering stricter measures against foreign interference. Proposals include monitoring social media to identify coordinated influence campaigns and identifying foreign actors behind operations. Such steps could also mean providing Swiss intelligence services with greater resources or powers to counter hybrid threats.

How to stay informed and verify sources

For the Swiss citizen, the context of increasing foreign interference means first of all remaining aware of the sources of information, especially during the voting campaigns. Checking the origin of articles and content on social media, checking if authors can be identified transparently, and distrusting particularly polarizing or emotional content is a fundamental information hygiene practice. Frequently Asked Questions
When does the Swiss neutrality vote take place?
The vote is set for 27 September 2026. Swiss citizens will decide whether to accept the initiative that would write into the constitution a broad ban on sanctions against states involved in armed conflict, with the exception of obligations to the United Nations.
What is RT.DE and why is it relevant?
RT.DE is a Kremlin-funded publication that published several articles during the campaign on Swiss neutrality. Some articles are critical of the Federal Council, which opposes the initiative, and favourable to the proposal. SRF reported that the newspaper is part of the Russian influence campaign on the Swiss vote.
What did the federal government say about foreign disinformation?
The State Secretariat for Security Policy (SEPOS) told SRF that foreign disinformation and state influence attempts specifically targeting Switzerland are on the rise. SEPOS warned that such operations may affect the results of elections and referendums. The Federal Council set up a working group at the end of 2025 to address this threat.
How can I protect myself from misinformation during a vote?
Always verify the provenance of articles and content online. Check if authors can be identified transparently and be wary of particularly polarising content. Consult official Swiss sources such as the Federal Chancellery portal and keep a critical eye on social media content, especially when it comes from unverified accounts.
Is Parliament considering new measures against foreign interference?
Yes. Parliament believes that the Federal Council should do more. The proposals include social media monitoring to identify influence campaigns, identification of foreign actors behind operations, and a potential increase in resources and powers for Swiss intelligence services.
