Swiss students read less: PISA 2025 results

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 8 September 2026

PISA 2025: Swiss fifteen-year-olds excel in science, but reading and mathematics fall. 28.8% do not achieve minimum skills. The investigation reveals the crucial influence of social origin.

Context

In Brief

  • PISA 2025: Switzerland declines in reading (-22 from 2015) and mathematics (-22)
  • Stable sciences and above OECD average: 501 points CH vs 482 OECD
  • Students with insufficient results growing: reading +8.8%, mathematics +7.4%
  • Social origin decisive: disadvantaged students report lower performance

Key Facts

  • What: International PISA 2025 survey on school performance of fifteen-year-olds
  • When: Results released Tuesday from the University of Bern
  • Where: Switzerland, on behalf of the Confederation and Cantons
  • Who: University of Bern, conducted on federal and cantonal behalf
  • Reading: 470 points (vs 492 in 2015), OECD average 461 points
  • Mathematics: 499 points (vs 521 in 2015)
  • Sciences: 501 points (stable, vs 482 OECD)
  • Students with insufficient results in reading: 28.8% (vs 20% in 2015)

Performance in natural sciences remains stable and above the OECD average, but students with insufficient results are growing in mathematics as well. On Tuesday, the University of Bern released the results of the PISA 2025 survey on Swiss fifteen-year-olds: data that confirms the strength of the national school system in some areas, but highlights a concerning decline in reading and mathematics.

In natural sciences, young Swiss students achieved an average of 501 points, positioning themselves clearly above the OECD average of 482 points. This result confirms the stability of performance since 2015, when the score was already high. In the same period, the OECD average has declined steadily, widening the gap in Switzerland's favor.

Reading and Mathematics in Decline

The picture changes significantly in reading and mathematics. In reading competence, the average value fell to 470 points from 2015, when it was 492 points. Despite the decline of 22 points, Switzerland is still above the OECD average of 461 points.

The negative trend repeats in mathematics: the score fell from 521 points in 2015 to 499 points in 2025. Here the loss of performance is even more marked: 22 points. However, Switzerland maintains a position of leadership at the international level in this area as well.

For model-based problem-solving, tested for the first time, Switzerland scored 520 points, a result above average. Moreover, in scientific-environmental competence, assessed for the first time in the survey, Switzerland achieved 495 points, always above the OECD average.

The Paradox of Low-Performing Students

Although the international positioning remains strong, the growing number of students with insufficient results is emerging. In reading, the share of young people who did not reach minimum competencies rose from 20% in 2015 to 28.8% in 2025: an increase of 8.8 percentage points in a decade. This phenomenon has significant implications for salaries and future economic prospects, given that lower skills correlate with lower incomes.

In mathematics, the trend is similar: the percentage of students with below-minimum performance has grown from 15.8% in 2015 to 23.2% in 2025, an increase of 7.4 percentage points. In natural sciences, instead, the share of young people with low performance remained stable at around 21%. This data reveals a polarization of the Swiss school system: while the best students maintain high levels of performance, a growing share of their peers falls behind in fundamental competencies.

Operational details

When compared to reference countries that are particularly relevant to Swiss educational policy, Switzerland consistently performs well. In natural sciences, only Estonia and Canada achieve better results in some sub-sectors. In mathematics, Switzerland stands out: only Estonia achieves even higher performance.

In reading, Estonia and Canada are ahead of Switzerland, while Finland and Italy reach a comparable level. This means that Switzerland, despite the decline, remains competitive with countries that historically excel in education. In model-based problem-solving, Switzerland is surpassed within the reference group only by Estonia, while Canada is statistically at the same level. Overall, Switzerland ranks among the top 20 countries out of 91 participants.

The Influence of Social Background

A central finding of the study is the major influence that social background still exerts on academic success. Students from socially disadvantaged backgrounds achieved significantly worse results on average compared to their peers from privileged backgrounds.

This gap between privileged and disadvantaged young people peaked in 2022 but returned to 2015 levels in 2025. However, this apparent stabilization masks a worrying dynamic: the improvement does not reflect actual support for disadvantaged youth, but rather a decline in performance even among privileged students. In other words, the system has declined overall, rather than lifting the most vulnerable.

Stability in Sciences, Decline in Reading and Mathematics

Stability in natural sciences contrasts sharply with declines in reading and mathematics. This suggests that the Swiss education system maintains strength in scientific fields, perhaps thanks to established curricula and methodologies, but has struggled in recent years to support basic linguistic and quantitative competencies.

The decline in reading is particularly significant given the central role of reading ability for success in all other disciplines. A student who cannot read fluently and with comprehension will face difficulties in mathematics, sciences, and humanities as well.

The increase in students with insufficient results implies a future challenge for Switzerland's labor market. Young people who today do not achieve minimum competencies risk encountering difficulties in finding qualified employment or progressing in the vocational training system.

Key points

The PISA 2025 data represent a snapshot of school performance at national and international levels. For Swiss parents, the message is twofold: on one hand, the school system maintains global competitiveness; on the other, the growing number of struggling students suggests that not all young people receive the support necessary to achieve basic competencies.

If your son or daughter struggles with reading or mathematics, it is not an anomaly, but rather part of a national trend. This means that resources for school support (tutoring, remedial programs, differentiated pathways) should be a priority in the Cantons. Swiss schools, while remaining competitive internationally, need targeted interventions to reverse the trend.

What Policy Makers Can Do

From the Confederation to the Cantons, these data represent a concrete call to action. Switzerland remains excellent in science and mathematics at the international level, but the downward trend in both areas requires attention. Policy makers will need to assess whether curricula are still suited to contemporary needs in reading and mathematics, whether teachers have updated methodologies, how to reduce the impact of social origin on performance, and what the plan is to reverse the negative trend by the next PISA cycle.

Switzerland is still among the 20 most performing countries of the 91 participants in the PISA 2025 survey. Maintaining this position requires strategic interventions and constant monitoring of the national educational trajectory.

Concrete Next Steps

If you are parents, monitor your child's progress in reading and mathematics; do not hesitate to contact the school if you notice difficulties; ask for information about support programs available in your Canton.
