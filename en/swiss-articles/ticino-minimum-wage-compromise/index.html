Minimum wage in Ticino: around 4'000 gross francs per month

The Grand Council of Ticino approved the compromise on the minimum wage on Monday. The report, published in April 2026, said that the agreement had been reached in the preceding weeks by the main parties. The cantonal Parliament accepted a counterproposal to the Socialist Party (PS) initiative entitled “For a Social Minimum Wage.” The approved approach therefore does not coincide with the initiative’s original text: the decision came through the compromise submitted to the Grand Council.

An agreement supported by almost everyone

The vote received the support of almost all political forces represented in Parliament. The convergence was not unanimous, however. Among those opposed, the Movement for Socialism described the agreement as a mere “adjustment for inflation of the minimum wage set in 2021.” The criticism points to a different interpretation of the compromise: for the MPS, the agreement went no further than updating the threshold already set, while the majority of political forces supported it as a solution reached after discussions in the preceding weeks.

The decision also had political significance linked to the socialist initiative. The compromise was its counterproposal, not its full approval. This distinction makes it possible to read the vote for what the source describes: an agreement between parties approved by the cantonal Parliament, after the initiative had opened the debate on the social minimum wage. The source does not attribute unanimous support to the vote, nor does it indicate that the initiative was accepted in its original wording.

The Director of the Department of Finance and Economy, Christian Vitta, said he was satisfied with the commission’s work. He described the result as a synthesis of different political viewpoints and referred to the various interests that, in his view, the rule must bring together: wage conditions, purchasing power, business competitiveness and, more generally, labor-market balance.

Vitta also presented the balance being sought as twofold: strengthening wage protection without forgoing rules that are sustainable and workable in practice. This is the rationale the State Councillor gave for the value of the compromise. The report does not describe the debate in the chamber in detail or give vote counts; it does, however, report both the support of almost all political forces and the MPS’s opposing position, as well as the positive assessment of the Director of the Department.

Operational details

A progression in three stages

Comparing the threshold in force at the time with the one planned once fully implemented makes it possible to calculate the change directly. The current range indicated by the source is 20-20,50 francs per hour; the range planned from 2029 is 21,75-22,25 francs. Subtracting the corresponding endpoints, both the lower and upper limits increase by 1,75 francs per hour. The width of the range, however, remains 50 cents: 20,50 minus 20 at the start, 22,25 minus 21,75 at the end.

The intermediate path spreads the change over time. From 2027, the range would rise to 20,50-21 francs per hour; from 2028, to 21-21,50; from 2029, to 21,75-22,25. The initial step compared with the current values adds 50 cents to both endpoints, as does the step from 2027 to 2028. Between 2028 and 2029, however, the difference between the corresponding endpoints is 75 cents. From 2030, the minimum wage would also be indexed to inflation. These are comparisons between hourly values reported in the source, not a forecast of how much each worker will receive in their paycheck.

The report pairs these ranges with a monthly estimate: compared with the current situation, the increase would exceed 300 francs per month and bring the minimum wage to around 4’000 gross francs. The source does not specify the number of monthly hours used to convert the hourly rates into a monthly amount. Therefore, the data make it possible to compare the hourly endpoints precisely and report the published monthly estimate, but not to independently calculate an exact monthly amount for every employment situation.

According to the report, the compromise would affect around 23’000 female and male workers: around 15’000 cross-border commuters and 8’000 residents. The two partial figures add up to the stated total. In the chamber, Socialist co-president Fabrizio Sirica said that two thirds of the beneficiaries are cross-border commuters and linked their presence to the composition of the workforce in some low-income sectors, where workers come almost exclusively from across the border. He also argued that the measure would have enabled residents to earn enough to live on and depend less on state assistance. These are the reasons given by Sirica, not a separate estimate from the source of the individual effects.

Key points

The issue of collective labor agreements

The compromise also concerned the hierarchy between the cantonal minimum wage and collective labor agreements (CCLs). The law then in force gave precedence to CCLs; the text approved by the Ticino Parliament instead stipulated that the minimum wage would prevail. In practice, the wages set out in the CCLs would have had to be adjusted and could not fall below the cantonal threshold. According to the text, the adjustment had to take place by 31 December 2029. The issue, therefore, is not just a matter of setting a minimum rate, but also of establishing which rule prevails when the collective agreement provides for lower pay.

The issue was at the center of the new socialist initiative. In 2015, Ticino voters had voted in favor of establishing a minimum wage. Subsequently, part of the business sector had tried to circumvent the law by creating TiSin, described by the source as a pseudo-union, and concluding CCLs with hourly wages below the minimum. The attempt was unsuccessful: in 2022, the Office of Labor Inspection had deemed the contracts signed by the association to lack legal basis. This precedent helps explain why the priority between the two thresholds was controversial, without adding assessments beyond what was reported.

At the federal level, the motion submitted by Council of States member Erich Ettlin was under discussion. The proposal stipulated that the minimum wages in CCLs declared generally binding should take precedence over cantonal ones. According to the April 2026 report, the Council of States had already approved the motion; a week earlier, the National Council's Economic Affairs and Taxation Committee had also said yes. The National Council was expected to take a position in turn, but the source did not report the outcome of that step.

The motion, as described in the text, provided for an exception for Geneva and Neuchâtel, which had already incorporated into their respective laws the principle that the minimum wage takes precedence over CCLs. For Ticino, the question remained open as to whether it could benefit from the same exemption, given that the new cantonal compromise reversed the precedence provided for under the previous law. Sirica had noted that two different legal interpretations existed. The report did not establish which one would prevail: any coordination between the cantonal rule and the federal one therefore depended on steps that, in the article published in April 2026, were still presented as unresolved.
