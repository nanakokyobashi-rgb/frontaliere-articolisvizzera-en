The 2026 Nobel Prize in Economics was awarded on Monday to Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford, both American professors: the former teaches at Stanford, the latter at Columbia University. The award recognizes their studies on the microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization.

The committee of the Swedish Academy explained that the models developed by the two economists have improved the ability to implement effective stabilization policies. The stated aim is twofold: to counter economic crises and keep inflation under control.

The committee’s rationale

The research focuses on how firms and households respond to government measures and monetary policy interventions. According to the committee’s explanation, expectations about the future can mean that even the mere announcement of a measure by governments or central banks influences economic behavior. For the committee, therefore, policy transmission also depends on the reaction of these actors, not only on the formal content of a decision.

The Swiss figure in the story is Ernst Fehr, a Swiss and Austrian citizen, a professor at the University of Zurich and considered a leading figure in behavioral economics. His studies have helped temper the image of “homo economicus,” conceived as an individual driven solely by self-interest. Considered among the candidates for many years, Fehr was once again left empty-handed.

In 2025, Joel Mokyr received half of the award, while Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt each received a quarter. The prize recognized their work on the role of innovation as a driver of economic growth, with a rationale distinct from the one chosen for 2026.

The economics prize closes the series; its presentation also takes place on December 10, the anniversary of Alfred Nobel’s death. However, it is not part of the original Nobel Prizes: it was established by the Royal Bank of Sweden in 1968, on the occasion of its three-hundredth anniversary.
