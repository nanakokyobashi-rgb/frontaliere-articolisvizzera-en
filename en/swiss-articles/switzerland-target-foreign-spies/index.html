The Hanning case and private companies

Kiriakou notes that foreign intelligence services are very active and cites the case of Switzerland, where numerous private intelligence companies operate. Some collect and analyze public data, such as information obtained from databases and the media, to market it to companies; others instead handle classified information.

To describe the phenomenon, the former agent cited a US company founded by a former CIA officer. Following the September 11, 2001 attacks, the company recruited numerous former agency employees as external consultants, becoming a company worth billions of dollars, active in the field of highly classified information.

According to Kiriakou, both types are also present in Switzerland. Hanning's board positions could have offered foreign intelligence services, especially Chinese or Azerbaijani ones, an opportunity to obtain information about Swiss activities. The hypothesis remains a possibility, however, not an established fact.

Despite the scandals that have involved Swiss intelligence in recent years, Kiriakou considers the Swiss intelligence service first-rate. In his view, the Confederation takes security, privacy and neutrality seriously. The tradition dates back to before World War II: intelligence gathering moved from the police to the Department of Defense and finally to the intelligence services.

His interpretation of the scandals does not erase the preventive work: intelligence services must prevent infiltration attempts and protect sensitive information. For an agent, recruiting a source with access to the data being sought remains one of the most coveted objectives.
