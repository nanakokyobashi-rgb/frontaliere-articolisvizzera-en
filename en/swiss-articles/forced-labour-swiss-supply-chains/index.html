  • On the other hand, the EU applies a 10% duty thanks to its existing legislation

Key facts

  • What: US investigation under Section 301 of the Trade Act
  • When: Announced on 12 March, results announced on 2 June
  • Where: Switzerland, subject to verification by the US sales representative
  • Who: Swiss Government, Economiesuisse, Swiss private sector
  • Amount: Additional flat-rate duty of 12.5% announced on 23 July

The United States launched an investigation against Switzerland on March 12 under Section 301 of the U.S. Trade Act, which concerns unfair or discriminatory trade practices. The aim was to check whether Switzerland was doing enough to prevent the import of goods produced through forced labour. The results were announced on 2 June and Switzerland was included in a list of 60 countries found to have failed to effectively introduce or enforce an import ban on goods produced with forced labour. The US trade representative said that Switzerland and other affected countries, by not effectively countering the phenomenon, would expose American producers to unfair competition.

Operational details

Switzerland-EU comparison on import ban

The fundamental difference between Switzerland and the European Union concerns the presence of specific legislation on the import of goods related to forced labour. While the EU already has a legal framework that explicitly prohibits the entry of such products into its internal market, Switzerland relies on a different system, aimed at prevention rather than customs blocking.

In Switzerland, forced labour is prohibited by domestic law and the country has adhered to ILO conventions, but there is no law that directly prohibits the import of goods produced in exploitative conditions. A bill is currently under discussion that, however, would only apply to about thirty large companies, leaving a large part of the national economic fabric uncovered.

This regulatory difference has concrete consequences at the commercial level. The United States has decided to apply an additional duty of 12.5% to Switzerland, while the EU applies one of 10%, thus recognizing the existence of Community legislation already in place. This difference of 2.5 percentage points translates into an additional burden for Swiss companies exporting to the US, particularly in the food, textile and technology sectors, where supply chains are often international and complex.

Key points

What can Swiss businesses do now

Swiss companies exporting to the US must act to reduce the impact of the new US duty. The first step is the complete mapping of your supply chain, identifying all direct and indirect suppliers involved in the production of goods for export.

Step 1: Supply Chain Mapping

Gather information on all actors involved: raw material suppliers, sub-suppliers, logisticians and producers. This process often requires collaboration with teams dedicated to sustainability or compliance.

Step 2: Risk Assessment

Apply assessment tools inspired by the OECD Guidelines to identify sectors and countries at higher risk of forced labour. Sectors such as agriculture, mining and textile manufacturing often require special attention.

Step 3: Implementing Preventive Measures

Develop codes of conduct for suppliers, conduct independent audits, and provide for anonymous reporting mechanisms for workers. These actions help demonstrate a concrete commitment to due diligence.

Step 4: Documentation and Transparency

Keep track of all actions taken and prepare reports to share with customers and customs authorities. Strong documentation can support any effort-based duty exemption or reduction requests

Frequently Asked Questions
Why did the US impose a 12.5% tariff on Switzerland?
The United States has imposed an additional flat-rate duty of 12.5% on Switzerland because the country does not yet have legislation explicitly prohibiting the import of goods produced with forced labour, unlike the European Union which already has such legislation and therefore suffers a lower duty of 10%.
What is the difference between the Swiss and European approaches to forced labour in imports?
Switzerland prohibits forced labour on its territory and has ratified international conventions, but does not have a law that blocks the import of goods produced with exploitation. The EU, on the other hand, has specific legislation that prohibits the entry of these products into its internal market, which is why the US applies a 10% duty against the Swiss 12.5%.
What do Swiss companies need to do to prepare for the new US duty?
Companies must map their supply chain, assess the risks of forced labour using tools inspired by the OECD Guidelines, implement preventive measures such as codes of conduct and audits, and document all actions to demonstrate compliance with US customs authorities.
