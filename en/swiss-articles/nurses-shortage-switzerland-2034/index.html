Nurses: shortage by 2034 in Switzerland (cross-border guide)

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 8 September 2026

The 4th Obsan report predicts a severe shortage of nurses by 2034. Only 55% of the needs are covered by personnel trained in Switzerland.

Context

In a nutshell

  • By 2034 aggravated shortage of nurses in Switzerland
  • Maximum coverage rate: 55% with trained Swiss staff
  • Early release: 75% graduates remain immediately, only 47% at 40 years of age
  • Two factors: 1/3 staff replacement + population ageing

Key facts

  • What: Nursing staff shortage by 2034
  • When: Screening period 2024–2034
  • Who: Swiss Health Observatory (Obsan)
  • Where: Swiss health institutions at national level
  • Coverage rate: 40%–55% for tertiary education in Switzerland
  • Early release: 75% after graduation, 47% at age 40
  • Report: 4th Obsan report published recently

Switzerland is preparing for an unprecedented health challenge. According to the 4th report of the Swiss Health Observatory (Obsan), the country will face an aggravated shortage of health personnel by 2034, with particular pressure on the nursing sector. The most critical data is immediate: the coverage rate of staff needs by those trained in Switzerland will not exceed 55% for nurses.

At the level of nursing care (tertiary), the projections indicate that the share of needs covered by training in the Confederation will be between 40% and 55% in the period 2024–2034. This time window is already upon us and the situation is getting worse every year.

Operational details

The alarm from unions: working conditions are the critical issue

Reactions to the Obsan report came quickly. The Swiss Association of Nurses (ASI) highlighted an unequivocal message: 'We must succeed in keeping qualified healthcare workers in the profession. For this, better working conditions are needed everywhere'. The Association sees in the Nursing Care Initiative, particularly in its second phase, 'a golden opportunity for political leaders to effectively combat the shortage and improve the framework conditions in a targeted way'.

The Swiss Public Services Union (VPOD/SSP) raised an even stronger alarm, describing the situation as 'concerning'. The VPOD does not ask for generic solutions, but concrete public investments to ensure sufficient staff, acceptable working conditions, and quality healthcare accessible to everyone. The message is clear: without addressing the problem of working conditions, no training program will be enough.

Why nurses leave so soon

The figures on early exits hint at the real underlying problem. Although the Obsan report does not analyze in detail the personal reasons, the statements from ASI and VPOD clearly suggest the factors: excessive workload, heavy shifts, salaries insufficient compared to responsibility and physical and psychological strain, and lack of professional recognition. The decline from 75% to 47% between the first year of work and 40 years of age indicates that 40% of nurses trained in Switzerland decide to completely change direction in their career.

Key points

What must change: the role of federal institutions, cantons and hospitals

The Obsan report provides the diagnosis; now concrete solutions are needed. At the federal level, the Federal Council and Parliament must recognize that the shortage of nurses by 2034 is not purely a demographic issue, but a crisis of sustainability in the national health system. This requires structural public investment, not just declarations of intent. Cantons play an even more central role: many directly manage hospitals and healthcare facilities and oversee professional training competencies at the tertiary level.

Improving nurses' salaries, reducing the number and intensity of shifts, investing in safer and more dignified work environments: these decisions fall to the cantons and healthcare institutions. The Nursing Care Initiative, of which the second phase represents the legislative tool to advance reforms, must become concrete and not remain on paper. Each canton must adapt measures to its own context, but the commitment must be federal.

Training and strategic international recruitment

Regarding the supply of healthcare professionals trained in Switzerland, nursing schools must be adequately funded. The fact that the coverage rate remains between 40% and 55% for the 2024–2034 period means that Switzerland will necessarily have to rely on international recruitment to fill the structural gap. This poses new challenges: integration of foreign professionals, formal recognition of foreign diplomas, management of language and working culture in Swiss facilities, and regulatory compliance with international agreements.

Frequently Asked Questions
What are the main causes of the expected shortage of nurses by 2034?
According to the 4th report of the Swiss Health Observatory (Obsan), two main factors are driving the crisis. First, health institutions will need to replace nearly a third of the staff leaving the sector by 2034. Secondly, the ageing of the population will lead to an increase in the need for additional nursing staff. Added to this is the phenomenon of early departures: only 47% of nurses remain in the sector at the age of 40, compared to 75% who work there immediately after training.
