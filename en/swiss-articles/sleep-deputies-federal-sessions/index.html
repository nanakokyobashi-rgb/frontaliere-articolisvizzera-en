Six hours and ten minutes of sleep per night: this is the average recorded during sessions by members of the National Council and Council of States. The figure, about 50 minutes below the usual duration, emerges from a study conducted at the University of Fribourg on 16 MPs and senators. Björn Rasch, 51, a sleep researcher and professor at the University of Fribourg, is the professional cited in the study. Blick reported on the study today.

The figure and the proposal

For Rasch, tiredness is not a secondary detail. Tired people, he explains, tend to make riskier decisions than those who are rested. Those who sleep less, he adds, also have more difficulty assimilating information. In a Parliament called upon to discuss and make decisions during sessions, the connection between sleep and performance therefore becomes the focus of his proposal.

The scholar calls for spaces at the Federal Palace for short naps during the day. The so-called power naps, according to Rasch, actually increase performance capacity. The solution, in his words, is not to leave parliamentarians sitting in the chamber, awake but tired: «If a parliamentarian fell asleep in the chamber, it would immediately be a scandal; leaving them sitting, awake but tired, is not a solution: why not create spaces for recovery?». A rest room would, in his view, be a pragmatic approach to maintaining politicians' efficiency.

Alcohol, sleeping pills and health

The quality of rest is also linked to alcoholic drinks often present at aperitifs on the sidelines of sessions. Alcohol can help one fall asleep, but in the second half of the night one wakes more often and remains awake longer. Sleeping pills help one fall asleep, without guaranteeing restorative rest; the study shows that only a minority of parliamentarians use them, in line with the general population.

Rasch argues that sleep should have greater priority in the national health strategy. From sleep disorders, he says, one can understand how stressed a person is; if they appear, the risk of burnout after two years increases. The researcher finally points to children and adolescents: when it comes to sleep, he concludes, more should be said at school. What can be gleaned from the data

The sample consists of 16 deputies and senators. The text does not present it as a snapshot of the entire population and does not offer separate results for groups, sessions, or contexts. The only comparison indicated is with the usual sleep duration of those same parliamentarians: about 50 minutes more than the six hours and ten minutes recorded during the sessions.

The distinction matters because the proposal does not present the nap as a convenience, but rather as a tool to support performance. At the same time, the source does not indicate an already implemented project, costs, a timetable, or an authority tasked with creating the room. Frequently Asked Questions
How much do parliamentarians sleep during federal sessions?
According to the University of Fribourg's survey of 16 deputies and senators, the average is 6 hours and 10 minutes per night during sessions, about 50 minutes less than their usual sleep duration.
What does researcher Björn Rasch propose to improve performance?
Rasch proposes providing spaces for short naps (power nap) at the Federal Palace, arguing that brief daytime rest increases performance capacity and that keeping parliamentarians awake but tired is not a solution.
What role do alcohol and sleeping pills play in the quality of parliamentarians' sleep?
Alcohol promotes sleep but causes more frequent awakenings in the second half of the night. Sleeping pills help you fall asleep without guaranteeing a restful rest; they are used only by a minority of parliamentarians, in line with the general population.
Why does Rasch call for greater priority to be given to sleep in the national health strategy?
Sleep disturbances signal elevated stress levels and, if they persist, increase the risk of burnout after two years. Rasch also suggests talking more about sleep at school for children and teens.
