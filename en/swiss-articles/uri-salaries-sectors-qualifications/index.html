The national framework for the labor market

In Switzerland, there is no federal minimum wage: this choice is decentralized to the cantons. Some Swiss cantons have adopted their own minimum wage regulations; for specific details in Uri, the competent cantonal administration (generally the cantonal economy/labor office) is the direct source. At the federal level, Swiss labor legislation sets standards on maximum hours, rest, holidays, and maternity/paternity leave that apply uniformly throughout the country.

The working hours are limited to 45 hours per week for industry, offices, and sales; for other sectors, the maximum is 50 hours per week. The minimum holidays are 4 weeks, except for workers under 20 years (5 weeks). Maternity leave amounts to 14 weeks at 80% of gross salary (managed through maternity allowance, IPG), while paternity leave is 2 weeks. The termination of the employment contract follows different periods: 1 month during the first year, 2 months from the second to the ninth year, and 3 months from the tenth year onwards.

Mandatory contributions and social security

On the salary front, the employee automatically pays mandatory contributions for social security and pensions. The AVS/AI/IPG (old-age and disability insurance and maternity allowance) amounts to 5.3% of the salary for the employee, while the employer pays an additional 5.3%, for a total of 10.6%. Unemployment insurance (AD) and insolvency allowance (AC) amount to 1.1% up to the annual maximum set by law. Accident insurance (LAINF, or LAA in French-speaking Switzerland) varies from 0.7% to 1.5% depending on the sector and professional risk, almost always at the employer's expense.

Professional pension (LPP, second pillar) is mandatory for incomes from the coordinated amount onwards (annual minimum threshold, indexed each year). LPP contributions on the coordinated salary vary by age class: 7% (25–34 years), 10% (35–44), 15% (45–54), 18% (55 years until the reference age). These amounts are deducted from the paycheck if the employer has joined a recognized pension fund. Tax-wise, Canton Uri applies a three-level system: federal direct tax (IFD), cantonal tax, and municipal taxes. Each municipality has its own multiplier that affects the cantonal rate, making the tax burden variable by municipality and making it essential to check the multiplier of your residence to estimate the total tax burden.

Operational details

Practical Implications for Those Working in Uri

Employees working in Uri, whether in the private or public sector, enjoy national Swiss rights guaranteed by federal legislation. The absence of a national minimum wage does not mean a lack of protection: collective labor agreements (CCL), when applicable to a sector, set negotiated minimum wages that often exceed legal minimums. Additionally, many Swiss cantons have introduced their own minimum wages; checking the website of the Uri cantonal administration is therefore essential for those entering the local job market to know if Uri has adopted specific cantonal legislation.

The maximum working hours of 45 hours per week constitute protection against overwork, with the right to weekly rest and daily breaks regulated by federal legislation and collective agreements. Those working beyond the maximum without compensation (paid overtime) or subsequent reduction in hours have the right to union or legal recourse with the cantonal labor office.

Deductions on Payslip and Net Estimate

An employee should expect a significant reduction in gross salary for mandatory contributions. In addition to social contributions (AVS/AI/IPG 5.3%, AD/AC 1.1%, LAINF approximately 1%), professional pension provision (LPP) has an even greater impact: a thirty-year-old contributes 7% of the coordinated salary (the salary net of a minimum coordination threshold, indexed annually), while a fifty-four-year-old contributes 15%. These amounts are automatic and disappear from the payslip if the employer has joined a recognized pension fund. Added to the social deductions are taxes: the federal direct tax is national, but the cantonal and communal tax varies by geographical location. In the Canton of Uri, each commune has a multiplier that affects the cantonal rate, creating significant differences in the final tax burden. An employee residing in a commune with a low multiplier will have a lower burden compared to one in a commune with a high multiplier, with the same gross income. The final net is often 60–75% of the gross, depending on age, sector, location, and income level. For a personalized estimate, using a salary calculator that includes the commune of residence in Uri is the most reliable method.

Collective Labor Agreements and Sectoral Protections

Collective labor agreements (CCL) are negotiated between unions and employers' associations for specific sectors and geographical regions. Not all sectors have a CCL, but where it exists, it sets minimum wages, hours, additional holidays, benefits, and conflict resolution procedures. In the Canton of Uri, consulting local unions (USS/SGB, Travail.Suisse, ASPO, and sectoral federations) or Uri employers' associations is the most direct way to know if your sector has a CCL and what wage levels it provides for level of education. Most Swiss CCLs provide progressive wage increases based on professional experience and educational title, ensuring protection even for the most vulnerable employees.

Key points

Procedure: check rights and salaries in Canton Uri

If you work or are looking for a job in Uri, here are the concrete steps to guide you through the local and national job market.

Step 1: Check the cantonal minimum wage

Consult the website of the Uri cantonal administration (Department of Economy, Labor Office, or Baudirektion). Search for the "Minimum Wage" section or equivalent terms in German/Romansh. Not all Swiss cantons have their own standard; if Uri has not adopted a legal cantonal minimum, the federal law applies de facto (no federal minimum, but protection via collective agreements where applicable to your sector). Local unions can also provide this information for free.

Step 2: Identify your collective agreement

The economic sector and company size determine whether a CBA is relevant to you. Swiss union federations (USS/SGB, Travail.Suisse, ASPO) and employers' associations have online CBA databases. Enter the sector name, region (Uri), and municipality; the result will tell you if there is a CBA, what minimum wages it negotiates, what additional holidays, and what complaint procedures. Many CBAs provide different minimum wages for apprentices, graduates, and degree holders.

Step 3: Calculate the estimated net

Use an online Swiss salary calculator to estimate how much of the gross will remain after contributions and taxes. Enter the proposed gross salary, Canton Uri, the municipality of residence in Uri, the fiscal year, and your date of birth (needed to calculate the LPP contribution by age class). The calculator will automatically deduct AVS/AI/IPG 5.3%, AD/AC 1.1%, LPP (7%, 10%, 15%, 18% by age class), LAINF (about 0.7–1.5%), federal direct tax, cantonal and municipal tax for the chosen municipality. The result shows the estimated monthly net and the breakdown of deductions.

Step 4: Check the tax multiplier of your municipality

The municipality multiplier where you reside directly affects the cantonal tax (which in turn affects the total along with federal and municipal tax). Visit the website of the Uri municipality of residence; the "Taxation", "Taxes", or "Fees" section reports the current multiplier (often indicated as "Steuermultiplikator" in German, "multiplicateur fiscal" in French). Low multipliers (e.g., 80) mean less tax burden; high ones (e.g., 150) mean more taxes at the same income. Comparing multipliers between municipalities helps assess the impact of residence.

Step 5: Know national rights on hours, holidays, and protections

The SECO (State Secretariat for Economic Affairs of the Swiss Federal Government) manages national information on maximum hours, rest, minimum holidays, maternity/paternity, and workers' rights. The seco.admin.ch website ("Rights and Obligations of Workers" section) contains guides in Italian, German, French. If you encounter violations of hours, holidays, or irregular termination, local unions offer free or low-cost advice on wage disputes, misconduct, and harassment. The Uri cantonal labor office is competent for inspections and reports of infringement.

Check the website of the Directorate of Economy or the URI cantonal labour office to verify. If URI has no legal minimum, the collective bargaining agreements (CLAs) applicable to your industry still set negotiated minimum wages, often higher than theoretical minimums. Is the maximum legal working time in Uri really 45 hours per week?
Yes, Swiss federal law sets a maximum of 45 hours per week for industry, offices and sales; for other sectors the maximum is 50 hours. These limits apply in URIs as well as across the country. Weekly breaks (at least 1 full day), daily breaks and the right to record hours are mandatory. If the employer imposes higher hours without compensation (paid overtime or subsequent rest days), the worker may report URIs for inspection to the local union or cantonal labour office.
How many weeks of holiday am I entitled to annually in URIs?
Swiss federal law guarantees a minimum of 4 weeks of annual leave (20 working days). Workers under the age of 20 are entitled to 5 weeks (25 days). Uri applies the same national minimum. Many collective and individual contracts guarantee more (5–6 weeks is common). Holidays not taken do not accrue indefinitely; the rules on liquidation depend on the individual contract or the applicable CCL.
How does the tax multiplier of the URI municipality affect my net salary?
The municipal multiplier directly affects the cantonal tax. A low multiplier (e.g. 80) reduces the tax burden; a high one (e.g. 150) increases it. For the same gross salary and age group, residing in a municipality with a low vs high multiplier can mean a difference of hundreds of CHF per month. Check the multiplier of your URI municipality on the municipal website (Taxation section). A custom salary calculator for your municipality shows the exact net impact.
