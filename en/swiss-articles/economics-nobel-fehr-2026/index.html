The winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Economics will be announced on Monday, October 12. Ernst Fehr, a Swiss-Austrian economist and professor at the University of Zurich, remains among the possible recipients.

The 70-year-old has been considered a possible winner for years, but is not among the leading favorites. Predicting the choice remains difficult: the Nobel Foundation keeps the names of the candidates secret, and decisions from previous years offer few clues about the committee's intentions.

The scientific profile

Fehr is one of the leading figures in behavioral economics, which examines the processes underlying individual economic decisions. His studies have helped cast doubt on the traditional model of “Homo oeconomicus,” which portrays individuals as guided exclusively by self-interest.

The studies cited by the source show instead that fairness, justice and the well-being of others also factor into choices. For this reason, Fehr is among the most cited economists in the German-speaking world.

Since 2012, he has headed the UBS International Center of Economics in Society, an institute affiliated with the University of Zurich and established thanks to an initial funding of 100 million Swiss francs from UBS bank. Fehr has assured that the institute's scientific independence is “completely guaranteed.”

His relationship with the Swiss university system also includes Università della Svizzera italiana: in 2009, USI awarded him an honorary doctorate.

His international reputation was on display last June: a conference held in Zurich to celebrate his 70th birthday was attended by several Nobel laureates, including Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, Joel Mokyr, Oliver Hart and Jean Tirole.

Clarivate did not include the economist among the names highlighted for 2026. The company selects researchers based on the scientific impact reflected in citations of their work; the source nevertheless specifies that the list does not constitute an official prediction of the award.

The picture therefore remains open, with Fehr still among the possible winners but outside the group of leading favorites. The decision will be made public only with the announcement scheduled for Monday, October 12.

Unlike the other five Nobel Prizes, this award was not provided for in Alfred Nobel's will.

Clarivate's list brings together names selected on the basis of scientific impact measured by citations. Therefore, Fehr's absence does not amount to a rejection, and the presence of the four economists does not amount to an official shortlist from the Nobel committee.

The second signal comes from a market in which users can bet on the award. Ariel Pakes, a Harvard economist specializing in empirical market analysis, is listed at 19%; Fehr is sixth at 6%. The source describes these assessments as speculative: they do not necessarily reflect the committee's decision.

For those reading the news from Switzerland, this distinction avoids assigning excessive meaning to a percentage. The 6% describes the position indicated by Polymarket at that moment, not an official figure from the Nobel Foundation nor an outcome already decided.

Key points

To follow the announcement without confusing candidacy, prediction, and result, a brief procedure can be applied. Its main purpose is to establish which information is already available and which remains to be verified.

A five-step check

1. Set the verification date. Monday, October 12 is the deadline indicated by the source for knowing the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Economics. Before then, use phrases such as possible winner or favorite, not winner.

2. Identify the origin of the names. If the list comes from Clarivate, read it as a selection based on publication citations, not as a communication from the Nobel Foundation.

3. Treat the percentages separately. If Polymarket appears, remember that the figures 19% and 6% are estimates from the prediction market: the first concerns Ariel Pakes, the second Ernst Fehr.

4. Check the language of the headline. The source allows Fehr to be described as a candidate or possible winner; it does not allow anticipating the committee's choice based on his position or the Clarivate list.

5. After the announcement, compare only the communicated outcome with the previous predictions. In this way, a market estimate and a citation-based selection remain different categories even when they point to the same name.
