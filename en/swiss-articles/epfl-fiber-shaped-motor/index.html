The prototype presented in Lausanne

The FiberMotor, a thread-like motor, was developed by a research group at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology Lausanne (EPFL). In a note, the institute specified that the device measures one to three millimeters in diameter. The description of the device was published in the specialist journal Advanced Materials.

The device consists of two hollow fibers, one positioned inside the other. Thin copper wires wrap around both. Applying an electrical voltage causes electrostatic forces to push them closer together. Consequently, the inner fiber moves back and forth inside the outer one.

Movement without gears

The construction distinguishes FiberMotor from the traditional motors described by the researchers. The device does not need gears and does not require mechanical transmission of force. For this reason, it is flexible and can also be moved from the outside. If, for example, an arm is moved in the opposite direction to that generated by the motor, the device does not lock up.

Four combined motors produced enough force to lift a 46-gram chocolate bar. This is the measurement reported for the test described by EPFL.

From the pants test to the start-up

One of the applications mentioned is a wearable robotic suit intended to help people walk. As a demonstration, the researchers integrated the motors into a pair of pants. The idea for the future is to insert many of these thin motors into clothing, making them work like threads. Together they could support movement when the leg bends or extends.

To bring the technology toward commercialization, the researchers have already founded a start-up. Initially, they want to make the motor more powerful and more durable. The source does not indicate a date for commercialization: the project is presented through the prototype, the pants test, and the development goals indicated by the team.

Three levels to distinguish

The first level is the form factor. A motor with a diameter of one to three millimeters can be described as thin; the source adds that it is flexible and can be moved from the outside. It is not merely a matter of reducing the dimensions: the absence of gears and a mechanical transmission allows the device to follow an opposing movement without locking up. The researchers link this characteristic to the possibility of inserting it into clothing like a thread.

The second level is the test. Four units lifted a 46-gram chocolate bar. This is a measurable result of the combined system, but it is not equivalent to describing a full suit. The source does not indicate how many units would be needed to support the movements of a leg, what operating time a garment would have, or what its price would be. How does the wire-shaped motor work?
