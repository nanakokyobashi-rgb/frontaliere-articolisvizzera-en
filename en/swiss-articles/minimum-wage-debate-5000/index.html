Minimum wage of 5,000 francs: the debate heats up

The debate on the labor market in Switzerland is heating up around the possible introduction of a 5000-franc minimum wage for workers who have completed an apprenticeship. Pierre-Yves Maillard, president of the Swiss Trade Union Confederation (SGB/USS), raised the issue in the columns of the newspaper Blick, launching a salary offensive aimed at guaranteeing a minimum pay threshold. The initiative has not yet been formalized as a concrete proposal; however, the topic is destined to occupy a central position during the 58th SGB/USS congress, scheduled for Friday and Saturday in Bern. The delegates present will evaluate the opportunity to launch a popular initiative, potentially during the coming year, to legally mandate a monthly salary of no less than 5000 francs for those holding a vocational training qualification.

The position of the SGB/USS and union motivations

According to Maillard, the need for this intervention arises from the fact that a salary must be sufficient to allow a worker to decently support a family, a condition that, in his view, occurs less and less frequently. The union's argument is based on an analysis carried out by the SGB/USS, using data from the Federal Statistical Office (FSO/BFS). The numbers show that 28% of workers with an apprenticeship earn less than 5000 francs per month, based on a 40-hour work week and including the thirteenth-month salary. The situation appears even more critical for 15% of workers, who earn less than 4500 francs per month. The president of the SGB/USS emphasizes how the salary gap between those who have completed an apprenticeship and university graduates continues to widen, defining this trend as an impoverishment that must be stopped. The debate, therefore, touches on the structural foundations of remuneration in Switzerland, highlighting a tension between the valuation of professional qualifications and current wage dynamics in the country's various economic sectors.

Operational details

The confrontation between trade unions and employers reveals divergent visions regarding wage management in Switzerland. While the SGB (Swiss Trade Union Confederation) focuses on a national minimum threshold to protect purchasing power, the Swiss Employers' Association (SAV) has expressed clear dissent regarding this proposal. The SAV particularly contests the rigidity of a measure that would not take into account the intrinsic diversities of the Swiss labor market. According to the employers' organization, value added, productivity, and salary levels vary significantly based on profession, economic sector, and geographical region. Therefore, the imposition of a single national minimum wage is considered by the SAV to be an undifferentiated solution that ignores the complexity of the Swiss economic system.

Analysis of wage and sector differences

The SGB argues that women are particularly penalized by the current wage structure. Based on the analyzed data, it emerges that one in three women with an apprenticeship earns a salary below 5,000 francs, while nearly one in five earns less than 4,500 francs per month. Professions cited as being most affected by low wages include retail, hairdressing, the service sector in general, and daycare centers. However, the issue is not limited exclusively to typically female professions; sectors where male presence is predominant, such as automotive mechanics or drivers, also present wage levels that the SGB defines as insufficient. The union analysis also highlights data regarding the real trend of wages: since 2016, the average salary of skilled workers has undergone a real contraction of 2.1%, a calculation that takes into account the effect of inflation. This figure is central to the debate, as it suggests that the loss of purchasing power for skilled workers is a constant phenomenon despite the profit growth recorded in other sectors of the economy. The SAV, on the other hand, counters by stating that those who have just completed an apprenticeship still need to gain the experience necessary to grow professionally, emphasizing the importance of allowing companies to recognize this development through salary progression based on skills acquired over time, rather than through rigid regulatory imposition that could limit labor market flexibility.

Key points

The difference of opinion between the social partners also extends to other demands made by the union, such as the introduction of at least six weeks of holidays during the apprenticeship period. USI has classified these requests as a re-proposal of already known requests, reiterating its firm opposition to legal provisions that excessively bind companies. The issue of the minimum wage is part of a national context where working conditions are often regulated by collective agreements that take into account the specificities of each sector. USI argues that flexibility is a key element in ensuring that companies can adapt to competitive challenges and variability in productivity across different Swiss cantons. The discussion, therefore, does not only concern the specific figure of 5000 francs, but the very model of regulation of the labor market.

Perspectives and limits of the current debate

It is important to point out that, at the moment, the proposal for a minimum wage of 5,000 francs remains a topic of political debate and not an existing law. There is no national legislation that sets a universal minimum wage of this amount for those who have completed an apprenticeship. The institutions involved, such as the USS and the USI, are outlining their positions in view of possible future developments, but no legal texts or official popular initiatives have yet been presented. Furthermore, no specific economic scenarios have been documented regarding the possible adaptation of existing collective labor agreements (CCL) in case of adoption of the minimum threshold. The debate remains confined to the level of the union proposal and the employer reaction, without any quantitative analyzes having been formulated on the macroeconomic effects or on the impacts for individual sectors in the event of implementation. Frequently Asked Questions
What is the proposal of the Swiss Trade Union Union (USS)?
The USS is considering launching a popular initiative to introduce a minimum monthly wage of 5000 francs for all workers who have completed an apprenticeship by law. The proposal, which aims to combat the impoverishment of skilled workers, will be the focus of the 58th USS congress in Bern. At the moment, this is a political debate and not an existing law.
What are the current wage figures for skilled workers?
According to data from the Federal Statistical Office (FSO) cited by the FSO, 28% of apprenticeship workers earn less than 5000 francs per month (calculated on 40 hours per week and 13 months' pay). 15% earn less than 4500 francs. In addition, the real wage of skilled workers has fallen by 2.1% since 2016 due to inflation.
Why is the Swiss Employers' Union (USI) opposed?
USI disputes the rigidity of an undifferentiated national minimum wage. According to the organization, such a measure would ignore the differences between sectors, regions and productivity levels. USI argues that flexibility is crucial for companies and that wage progression should be based on the acquisition of experience and skills, rather than on rigid legislative imposition that would limit the competitive capacity of companies.
