The 11.00 news bulletin on October 21, 2026 reports that the information theft affecting Publica is more serious than expected. Akira’s hackers obtained the personal data of nearly all federal employees of the Confederation: over 100’000 people, including both those still working and those no longer employed by the Confederation.

The list includes officials, judges and even secret agents, as well as former federal councillors. The cyberattack targeted an external provider and dates back to late September. It had already been known for a couple of weeks, but its true scale emerged on Tuesday through articles published by media outlets in the Tamedia group.

The data and the hackers’ demand

The information in the criminals’ possession includes contact details — addresses and phone numbers —, personal details such as names and dates of birth, salary data and additional information concerning partners and children. The source does not describe a simple contact list: it brings together personal details, contact details, remuneration and family-related data.

The hackers are reportedly demanding payment of a ransom. Otherwise, they threaten to publish or sell the data. Publica has reportedly not yet informed those affected about the payment demand; in the meantime, some have already been targeted by attempted scams.

The case can be viewed through two dimensions that the report makes explicit: who is included in the group and what types of information were stolen. The second goes beyond basic identification: alongside names appear dates of birth, addresses, telephone numbers and salary data.

The sequence of events in the case and Akira's precedents

The service dates the cyberattack to the end of September and reports that it had already been known about for a couple of weeks. On Tuesday, articles published by the Tamedia group's outlets made its actual scope clear. The news report does not provide a precise day for the attack and does not identify the external provider affected: these are the limitations of the available chronological and operational reconstruction.

The passage concerning communication with the people affected is also documented. Regarding the payment demand, Publica has reportedly not yet informed those concerned. The report does state, however, that some people have already been targeted by attempted scams. It does not indicate how many there are or describe the technique used in the attempts.

The precedent in Switzerland

Akira has already carried out attacks in Switzerland, including against private companies. A year ago, the federal authorities urged companies not to comply with the hackers' demands. A few months later, RUAG, owned by the Confederation, had opted to pay, following Akira's attack against its US subsidiary.

This precedent offers a documented but limited comparison: it concerns RUAG and its US subsidiary, not Publica. In the case of the Confederation's pension fund, the source speaks of a ransom demand and threats of publication or sale, but does not indicate the amount requested or any decision on payment.

The material reported by the news service thus makes it possible to clarify the groups involved, the categories of data listed, the presence of a ransom demand and the precedent of RUAG. It does not, however, make it possible to add a numerical breakdown of the groups concerned, details about the external provider or further information about the attempted scams.
