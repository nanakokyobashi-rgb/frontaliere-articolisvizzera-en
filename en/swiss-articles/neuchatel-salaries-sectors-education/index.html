Wages and the labour market Neuchâtel: sectors and training (cross-border guide)

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 7 September 2026

Salaries, hiring sectors and collective agreements in the Canton of Neuchâtel: how the Swiss system of salaries by level of training and contributions works.

Context

In brief

  • In Switzerland, there is no federal minimum wage: some cantons, such as Neuchâtel, establish it
  • Salaries vary significantly depending on the level of education (CFC, high school diploma, university)
  • Each employee contributes 5.3% to AVS/AI/IPG; the employer contributes 10.6% (total 15.9%)
  • Collective agreements define salaries and working conditions in major sectors

Key facts

  • What: Swiss salary system based on education level and collective agreements
  • Where: Canton of Neuchâtel and the Swiss national system
  • Who: Employees, employers, cantonal labor offices
  • Hours: Max 45 hours/week (industry, offices, sales) or 50 hours in other sectors
  • Contributions: 5.3% AVS/AI/IPG for the employee, 10.6% for the employer
  • Vacations: Minimum 4 weeks per year (5 if under 20)
  • Maternity: 14 weeks at 80%

In the Canton of Neuchâtel, as in the rest of Switzerland, the labor market operates according to well-defined federal and cantonal rules. Unlike many European countries, Switzerland does not have a federal minimum wage, but some cantons — including Neuchâtel — have established their own. The Swiss system for determining salaries is primarily based on three factors: the level of professional training, work experience, and collective labor agreements (CCL) in effect in the sector.

Education levels significantly influence the salary range. Those who have completed an apprenticeship (Federal Vocational Baccalaureate, CFC) access different salaries compared to those with a high school diploma or university education. Each sector is often regulated by collective agreements that set minimum wages, annual increases, risk allowances, and working conditions.

Operational details

Salary levels by education level

Education plays a central role in determining salary in Switzerland. A worker with a professional training certificate (CFC) typically earns a lower salary than someone with a high school diploma or advanced technical training. Those who have completed a university degree access even higher salary brackets. The difference is not marginal: it can exceed 40-50% between a CFC holder and a university graduate.

In the Canton of Neuchâtel, as in other Swiss cantons, there is no single national salary table. Salaries are determined by collective labor agreements (CCL) specific to each sector. These contracts are negotiated between unions and employers' associations; they set the minimum salary for each category, annual progressions, special allowances, and protections against dismissals.

Collective agreements and cantonal minimum wage

Collective agreements are the main instrument of wage protection in Switzerland. Unlike a rigid federal minimum wage, CCLs allow flexibility by sector and region. Some sectors (construction, retail, metal industry) have almost nationally binding CCLs; others have regional or company agreements.

In the Canton of Neuchâtel, the cantonal minimum wage — where provided by local legislation — defines the minimum threshold below which an employer cannot go. This applies regardless of the applicable CCL. If a CCL sets a minimum higher than the cantonal minimum wage, the CCL prevails. If no CCL applies and the employer offers less than the cantonal minimum, the contract is null and void on that part.

Key points

How to verify if your salary is correct

If you work in the Canton of Neuchâtel, the first step is to consult the collective agreement applicable to your sector. You can search for the current collective agreements at the Neuchâtel cantonal labor office or on the websites of Swiss employers' associations and trade unions. Compare your gross salary with the minimum required by the collective agreement and the cantonal minimum wage: it must be at least equal to that.

If your salary is lower, you are entitled to a dispute with the employer. Before taking legal action, seek an amicable solution; many cantons offer free mediation services. If the employer does not pay what is due, you can contact the cantonal mediation office or a union specializing in labor rights.

The SECO (State Secretariat for Economic Affairs) publishes median salaries by sector and level of education on a national scale. The UST/BFS (Federal Statistical Office) offers data and surveys on Swiss salaries divided by canton, sector, age, and gender. These tools are public and free.

Many Swiss unions provide online salary calculators, where you can enter your level of education, sector, age, and region to get an estimate of the average salary. This allows you to assess whether you are paid in line with market conditions.

Next steps

If you are looking for a new job in the Canton of Neuchâtel, use the SECO and BFS data to negotiate an adequate salary. If you are already an employee and suspect a breach of contract, contact the cantonal labor office.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the federal minimum wage in Switzerland?
Switzerland does not have a federal minimum wage. Only a few cantons have established it by law. In the Canton of Neuchâtel, if provided for by local legislation, the cantonal minimum wage must be respected by employers irrespective of the collective agreement. For specific details, check with the cantonal labour office in Neuchâtel.
How much does health insurance (LAMal) cost against my salary?
Health insurance (LAMal/KVG) is not a salary deduction, but a private policy that pays directly. The premium varies by canton, age group and chosen deductible (from CHF 300 to CHF 2500 per year). The Canton of Neuchâtel offers subsidies to the lowest incomes. No amount is withheld from your paycheck; you receive a separate invoice.
What are the mandatory contributions deducted from my paycheck?
From each paycheck are deducted: AVS/AI/IPG (5.3%), AD/AC unemployment insurance (1.1% up to the annual maximum), LAINF/LAA accident insurance (0.7–1.5% according to sector), and if enrolled in a pension fund (LPP/BVG), contributions ranging from 7% to 18% depending on age. The employer in turn pays 10.6% for AVS/AI/IPG.
How many weeks of vacation am I entitled to?
In Switzerland the legal minimum is 4 weeks a year for workers over 20; it rises to 5 weeks if you are under 20. Many collective and individual agreements offer additional holidays. In case of maternity, you receive 14 weeks of allowance at 80% of salary; paternity leave is 2 weeks.
What should I do if my salary is lower than the minimum provided for in the collective agreement?
First, check the CCL applicable to your sector at the cantonal labour office or your union. If your salary is lower, report it in writing to the employer asking for recovery. If the employer does not respond positively, contact the cantonal conciliation office or a trade union. Always keep a copy of your paycheck and individual contract.
