The September decision

On September 24, the Swiss National Bank kept its policy rate at 0 percent. According to the summary of the monetary policy discussion published Thursday, the Governing Board of the BNS does not currently consider it necessary to increase its readiness to operate in the foreign exchange market.

The document concerns the September rate decision and makes explicit the change in wording compared with June. At that time, the BNS was still declaring a greater readiness to operate in the foreign exchange market. In September, it removed that indication, considering it no longer necessary in the current context.

The rationale indicated in the minutes is limited in scope. The BNS notes only a slight increase in medium-term inflationary pressure and considers the second-round effects related to the rise in energy prices barely perceptible. So far, the summary adds, there are few signs that the increases are being passed on to other goods and services or to wages.

The reference to oil adds a specific condition to the assessment: the longer prices remain at high levels, the greater the risk of second-round effects. The BNS does not describe these effects as already widespread; it presents them as barely perceptible, with few signs of transmission to other goods and services or to wages.

The reference to foreign-exchange readiness therefore describes the central bank's assessment of the need to be ready to intervene. The news concerns both the policy rate, which remained at 0 percent, and the language the BNS uses to assess its position on foreign exchange. The minutes also open a second chapter, devoted to the possible effects of artificial intelligence on the labor market, still without a definitive assessment.

Key points

AI appears in the minutes

In addition to prices and exchange rates, the SNB discussed the possible effects of artificial intelligence on the labor market. According to the summary, the spread of AI could result in higher structural unemployment and a slowdown in consumption. The document does not, however, offer a definitive assessment: it is still too early for a conclusive evaluation.

This passage has a different scope from the one on energy inflation. In the first case, the SNB describes still limited signs that energy price increases are being passed on to other goods, services and wages. In the second, it sets out a possible development linked to the evolution of AI: higher structural unemployment and weaker consumption. The source does not establish that these effects have already occurred, nor does it link them to an immediate monetary policy measure.
