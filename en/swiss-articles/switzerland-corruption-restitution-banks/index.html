Key Facts

  • What: Return of funds from corruption
  • When: September 2026
  • Where: Switzerland, federal financial center
  • Who: Swiss banks and federal authorities
  • Problem: Complex process; lack of obligation for direct payment to States

As of September 2, 2026, the issue of returning money from corruption remains one of Switzerland's Achilles' heels. The process is known for being complex and lengthy, raising a fundamental question about Swiss banking regulation: why doesn't the State impose on banks to pay these sums directly to the States that have been harmed?

The question reveals a tension between the regulatory role of federal authorities and the operational methods of Swiss credit institutions. Banks manage significant flows of money, including those related to anti-corruption proceedings. However, the absence of an explicit obligation for direct payment to damaged countries suggests a regulatory framework still evolving, where the balance between national sovereignty and international compliance remains delicate.

Operational details

The perspective of federal regulation

The regulation of Swiss banks falls under federal control, primarily through institutions such as FINMA (Federal Financial Markets Supervision Authority) and SNB (Swiss National Bank). These authorities supervise compliance with anti-corruption and compliance regulations. However, the current regulatory framework does not provide for an explicit obligation to directly transfer funds to countries harmed by corruption offenses.

Why this gap? The reasons can be multiple: legal complexity, state sovereignty in fund management, parallel civil proceedings, or political considerations. Switzerland has a long tradition of neutrality, which also influences decisions on the management of international funds. At the same time, the Swiss financial market must maintain global credibility in a context where transparency and compliance are increasingly essential.

The issue raised in September 2026 is not new, but receives renewed attention for the central role that transparency and banking compliance play in international commercial relations. Switzerland's trading partners, particularly the European Union and the United States, exert constant pressure for stricter regulations on the return of illicit funds.

Implications for Swiss banks and institutions

Swiss banks operate in an increasingly rigorous compliance context. Even without an explicit obligation to transfer directly, they must comply with anti-money laundering (AML), Know Your Customer (KYC) and enhanced due diligence regulations. Every transaction related to potentially illicit funds is subject to strict verification.

Key points

What to do if you want to understand the Swiss compliance system

If you work in the financial sector, are an international entrepreneur, or simply want to better understand how Switzerland addresses corruption, various information paths are available. First: consult FINMA's official guidelines on the website of the Federal Financial Market Supervisory Authority. FINMA publishes circulars, standards, and recommendations on banking compliance. Second: contact your Swiss bank directly, which has dedicated teams for regulatory compliance matters.

Third: follow federal parliamentary debates. Although the repatriation issue is mainly technical, the Federal Council and Parliament periodically revisit regulations on the management of illicit funds. These discussions can lead to reforms that simplify the repatriation process. Fourth: for those operating in high-risk sectors (import/export, construction, public procurement), anti-corruption due diligence is mandatory. Documenting your suppliers, beneficial owners of contracts, and financial flows is essential.

Concrete steps for solid compliance

If you hold an account at a Swiss bank or manage international transactions:

1. Verify your bank: ensure that you operate according to the highest compliance standards. Swiss banks are subject to constant FINMA supervision.

2. Documentation: keep all documentation relating to your transactions. In case of investigations into potentially illicit funds, traceability is essential.

3. Accurate declarations: complete tax declarations with maximum precision, indicating all sources of income and international assets.

4. Specialized advice: if involved in complex cross-border transactions, consult experts in banking compliance and anti-corruption regulations.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is meant by money from corruption?
It is illicit capital arising from bribery, fraud, embezzlement of public funds or other corruption offences. It is often transferred to countries with banking systems considered stable, including Switzerland. Swiss banks are required to identify and report such transactions through anti-money laundering (AML) procedures. Detecting money from corruption remains a technical and administrative challenge involving legal audits and international coordination.
Why is the return of corruption money to the injured States a long and complex process?
Return requires multiple steps: identification of the illegal origin, blocking of accounts, international legal checks, diplomatic negotiations between governments, judicial decisions and finally the actual transfer. Each phase involves different institutions (banks, Swiss federal authorities, foreign governments) and requires coordination. In addition, issues of legal jurisdiction and national sovereignty must be resolved, which prolongs the overall timeframe.
What does it mean that Switzerland does not oblige banks to pay the sums directly to the injured states?
Currently, the Swiss regulatory framework does not provide for an explicit obligation for banks to directly transfer seized funds to corrupt states. Instead, the process goes through federal authorities, legal proceedings, and often bilateral negotiations. This creates additional administrative complexity that prolongs return times and generates debate about the possibility of simplifying the system.
What are your obligations if you have an account with a Swiss bank?
You are required to accurately complete any returns relating to your assets and sources of income. Your bank performs Know Your Customer (KYC) checks and enhanced due diligence to identify any suspicious transactions. If your transactions involve high-risk countries or sensitive sectors, you will be subject to additional controls. Keep all the supporting documentation of your cash flows.
How can citizens verify that their bank complies with anti-corruption regulations?
You can consult the reports and communications of FINMA (Federal Financial Market Supervisory Authority). Contact your bank directly for information on their compliance, due diligence and anti-money laundering procedures. Major banks publish sustainability reports that include sections on governance and regulatory compliance. For complex international transactions, rely on consultants specialised in banking compliance and anti-corruption.
