Wages and work in Schaffhausen: sectors, training, levels

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 6 September 2026

Wages in the Schaffhausen: levels for training, collective agreements and social contributions in the Swiss system.

Context

In brief

  • Switzerland has no federal minimum wage; some cantons have their own
  • Salary levels depend on education, sector, and collective agreements
  • Mandatory social contributions: AVS/AI 5.3%, LPP 7% to 18%
  • Maximum working hours 45-50 hours/week; minimum 4 weeks vacation

Key facts

  • What: Swiss salary determination system based on education, sector, and collective agreements
  • When: Current system; amounts indexed annually
  • Where: All cantons, including Schaffhausen
  • Who: Employees in the private, public, and semi-public sectors
  • Regulation: Federal labor law, cantonal ordinances, collective agreements
  • Working hours: 45 hours/week (industry, offices, sales); 50 hours other sectors
  • Vacation: Minimum 4 weeks; 5 weeks until age 20

The Swiss system: no federal minimum wage

Switzerland is one of the few European countries without a mandatory federal minimum wage. This means that the Canton of Schaffhausen, like every other canton, determines autonomously whether and what minimum wage to set. Some Swiss cantons have introduced their own cantonal minimum wage; others do not. Salary determination follows criteria related to professional training, experience, and collective labor agreements (CCL), which regulate salaries for sector and profession.

In Switzerland, the labor market recognizes three main levels of education: vocational training with a federal certificate (EFA), high school diploma (which allows access to university), and university education (bachelor and master). Each level corresponds to different salary scales. A professional with EFA earns on average less than someone with a high school diploma, who in turn earns less than a degree holder. The differences are not fixed but vary by sector.

Collective agreements are the main tool for salary regulation. Negotiated between unions and employers' associations, CCLs set minimum wages, seniority increases, benefits, and working conditions for entire sectors (construction, trade, industry, public services, etc.). In the Canton of Schaffhausen, as in every canton, the current CCLs reflect local compromises between trade unions and business organizations.

Working hours and vacations

The federal labor law sets the maximum working hours at 45 hours per week for the industrial, office, and sales sectors, and at 50 hours for other sectors. The minimum vacation is four weeks per year for adults, rising to five weeks until age 20. These limits apply uniformly in all cantons.

Operational details

Salary differences by level of education and sector

The Swiss job market rewards education, but the starting salary of a new hire also depends on the sector. Manufacturing, finance, technology, and healthcare tend to offer higher salaries. Retail, hospitality, and agriculture generally pay less. In Schaffhausen, as in the rest of Switzerland, traditional economic sectors (industry, craftsmanship) remain important, and wages follow national levels for their branch.

A generic scenario illustrates the differences: a new hire with a Federal Vocational Baccalaureate (FVB) in the manufacturing sector receives a base salary linked to the collective labor agreement (CLA) of their branch, possibly increased by performance bonuses. Those who have completed a high school diploma can access administrative or technical positions with higher salaries. A university graduate generally starts with an even higher salary, although this depends on the specialization and sector. However, these differences are not automatic: a graduate in a poorly paid sector (e.g., cultural animation) might earn less than a specialized FVB technician in a high-demand sector (e.g., construction).

Pay slip structure: contributions and deductions

The Swiss pay slip shows the gross salary and all mandatory deductions. The main social contributions are:

  • AVS/AI/IPG: 5.3% on the gross salary (paid by the employee). The employer contributes an additional 5.3%.
  • Unemployment insurance (AD): 1.1% up to an annual maximum.
  • Accident insurance (LAA/LAINF): 0.7–1.5% depending on the sector (paid by the employer; generally not deducted from the pay slip).
  • Occupational pension scheme (LPP/BVG): 7% (for coordinated salary, age bracket 25–34 years), increasing with age: 10% (35–44 years), 15% (45–54 years), 18% (55+ until retirement age).
  • Health insurance: Voluntary and private, with premiums varying by canton. It is not a salary deduction but a personal expense.

These amounts are strictly regulated by federal law and cantonal ordinances. The rates are annually indexed, so the exact values vary each year. Anyone reading a Swiss pay slip should expect a significant difference between gross and net due to these mandatory factors.

Impact of pension scheme on salary

An often underestimated aspect is the weight of the occupational pension scheme (LPP). For an employee between 25 and 34 years old, 7% of the coordinated salary (total salary minus a standard deduction) goes into LPP. This represents a considerable value in the medium to long term, as it forms the basis of the pension benefit. As age advances, the contribution percentage increases, reflecting the concept that those who enter the pension scheme later must contribute higher percentages to achieve an equivalent benefit. Understanding this mechanism helps to truly evaluate the value of a Swiss salary beyond just the monthly net amount.

Key points

Inquiring about salaries: where to look

The first step for anyone entering the Swiss job market is to understand the salary range of their sector and region. There are several free public resources:

1. Federal Statistical Portals (FSO/BFS): The Federal Statistical Office publishes annual data on average wages by economic sector, canton and level of education. This data is free and reports the real market situation.

2. Collective agreements: CCLs are published by trade unions and employers' associations. Consulting them before accepting a job offer allows you to check whether the proposed salary complies with the industry's negotiation standards.

3. Cantonal labor offices: In the Canton of Schaffhausen, as in every canton, there is a cantonal office responsible for the labor market. These offices provide information on the validity of collective agreements, cantonal minimum wages (if they exist) and workers' rights.

4. SECO national portal: The State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) publishes a database of current collective agreements. You can search by canton and sector to check the terms in force.

Procedure for evaluating a job offer

When you receive a job offer in Switzerland, follow these steps:

  • Check the proposed gross salary against the UST/BFS data and the current CCL for the sector.
  • Request a simulation of your net paycheck using a calculator (available on the site) to really understand how much you will receive considering all contributions.
  • Check the cancellation terms: the contract must respect the legal terms (1, 2 or 3 months depending on the period of employment already completed).
  • Read the contract carefully to check additional benefits (hours, flexibility, bonuses, extra leave days).

Understanding Swiss salary levels avoids surprises and allows for informed negotiation. In Schaffhausen, as in the rest of Switzerland, the job market is transparent and regulated: it pays to know the rules.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the minimum wage in Schaffhausen?
Switzerland does not have a federal minimum wage. The Canton of Schaffhausen determines independently whether to establish its own cantonal minimum wage. The salaries are mainly regulated by collective agreements (CCL) negotiated between trade unions and employers' associations for each sector. To know the minimum wage in force in Schaffhausen (if any) and the terms of the CCLs in your sector, consult the cantonal labour office in Schaffhausen or the SECO database of collective agreements in force.
How does salary vary based on training?
The Swiss labour market recognizes three levels of training: federal certificate (AFC), high school graduation, and university education. On average, those with an AFC earn less than those who have completed a degree, which in turn earns less than a college graduate. However, industry and experience greatly influence pay scales. An AFC specialist technician in a required sector (e.g. construction) could earn more than a graduate in a less lucrative sector.
What are the main contributions deducted from the paycheck?
The main contributions are: AVS/AI/IPG at 5.3% of gross salary (total payment with employer: 10.6%), unemployment insurance (AD) 1.1% up to annual ceiling, accident insurance (LAA) 0.7-1.5% by sector. Occupational pension (LPP) varies with age: 7% (25–34 years), 10% (35–44), 15% (45–54), 18% (55+ up to the reference age). Health insurance is private, not deducted in the paycheck. All these amounts are indexed annually.
How do I check if a salary is compliant?
Compare the offer against three sources: UST/BFS (Federal Statistical Office) data for the sector and canton, the collective agreement in force for your branch (available in the SECO database), and union consultants or cantonal labour offices. These tools allow you to understand if the offer is in the market range and complies with the negotiating terms of your sector.
What is the maximum working time in Switzerland?
The Federal Labor Law sets the maximum hours at 45 hours per week for the industrial sector, offices and sales, and at 50 hours for other sectors. The minimum holiday is four weeks a year for adults, rising to five weeks until the age of twenty. These limits apply in all cantons, including Schaffhausen.
